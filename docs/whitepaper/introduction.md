# Introduction
Much of the media speaks in unison, and often no one has ordered it. Writers fall in line because disagreeing costs them too much. First, there are only a handful of places to publish, so a writer who falls out with one has few others to turn to. Second, leaving usually means leaving their work behind. Most writers find it safer to go along. Polyphony addresses both. First, anyone can start a publication with little effort, alone or with others. Second, writers keep their work when they leave. With more publications competing for writers, a writer who disagrees has somewhere to go, and the media has less reason to speak with one voice.

Three situations show the problem as writers and editors meet it today. Two journalists work a beat together for years: the same sources, the same enemies, a running series of investigations that readers cite by the site's name rather than by either byline. The site belongs to one of them. Half the archive is the other's work. Then a question arrives that splits them, and the other says so in public. Shortly after, he is gone. He says he was fired for dissent; the owner says he was let go for cause. Whichever is true, years of his work still stand on the site, under the masthead of the owner he is accusing, and whether they stay, come down, or quietly change bylines depends on the owner's goodwill, not on anything he controls.

A group of writers runs a publication on a platform that promised to stay out of the way. It does, including when it starts carrying, and taking a cut from, writing that some of the group cannot stand beside. Half want to leave at once. Half say the audience is here, and principles don't pay rent. The email list can be exported. The rest cannot: the paying readers, who found them through the platform's own recommendations, and the years of links pointing at the platform's address. Whoever leaves takes a list and starts over. Whoever stays keeps the rest. The disagreement was about the writing. The split is about the platform's terms, which none of them set and none of them can change.

A war breaks out, or a law passes that divides a country, and an editor sets out to publish the widest range of serious views on it, side by side, under one name. The invitations go out. Some say yes at once, mostly people with little to lose, whose positions are already known. Others hesitate. They don't yet know who else will be in it, and once their piece is in, it stays beside whatever arrives next. If one contribution later becomes unbearable, leaving means asking the editor to take their piece down, and the editor can agree, delay, argue or refuse. A writer who is refused has one lever left, which is to go public, and then the withdrawal becomes the story. So the most careful writers stay out, and the range the editor wanted narrows before the first piece appears.

In all three cases, the disagreement is settled by something other than the writing: by who owns the infrastructure. Writers and editors who publish together have two options today, and both end there. They can join a platform, whose terms can change or end without them. Or they can host their own site, which puts the site, the subscriber list, the archive and the revenue in the hands of whoever holds the credentials. Editors lose too, because the careful writers they most want stay out. What accumulates is not a policy but a habit, and writers learn to either align or stay out. The result is a publication that speaks with one voice and can point to no instance of having enforced it.

Polyphony is a protocol, not a platform. Publishing has always had three parties: the author, the publication, and the press that turns a manuscript into something readers can see. The relationship was never equal. The author could not take a manuscript to the press directly. The publication controlled the relationship with the press, and much of its power over the author came from that. Online, the press is the server, the publishing software or the platform. Polyphony moves the press into a neutral position between the two.

Its press is open code running on sealed hardware that not even its owner can see into. It decrypts a manuscript, renders the page and signs it with its own key, and only while an agreement covers that manuscript, so neither the author nor the publication can produce a valid page alone. Nor can either side act on the other's work: either can end an agreement without the other's consent, and no one but the author can change what the author wrote. What is left is trust: in that hardware, and in the people who own it not to collude. Section 6 says how that trust can be raised.

The rest follows from that. The author's manuscripts are stored encrypted, and only the press can open them: for the author at any time, for a publication's reviewers while they consider one, and for building pages only while an agreement covers it. Readers see the finished pages, never the manuscript. The author shares a manuscript with a publication under an agreement. Each agreement is recorded on a public ledger that neither party operates, so ending one is a fact, not a request. Once the author ends their agreement, the manuscript can no longer be decrypted for the publication's pages, and the author, or anyone acting for the publication, can have the site rebuilt without it, so what readers see follows the agreements in force. No person stands in between who could delay, argue or refuse.

Because neither the author nor the publication controls the press, its signature means something. Every page it produces carries that signature, and points to the manuscript, which the author signed, and to the agreement on the ledger. Together they show who wrote the page, that author and publication agreed to publish it, and that the copy in front of the reader is unchanged since it was rendered. The author is identified by an account, which may be a pseudonym, but the account is public, and every page published under it points back to it. So a byline becomes something a reader can check, and a reputation becomes something an author builds over time and no one else can claim. That matters more as machine-written text becomes cheap and plentiful. The protocol cannot tell whether a person or a model wrote the words. It can show who stands behind them, and that, over time, is what a reader has to go on.

It is built for places where the threat to editorial independence is less the state than whoever owns the infrastructure. It is no defence against a state-level adversary: a government that goes after a journalist can compel the parties to any arrangement, this one included. Nor does it decide the business model, or how its own machinery is governed. Payment is up to each publication: the protocol leaves room for any model, and offers rails on which revenue reaches the author directly instead of being passed on by the publication. Governance is up to each implementation of Polyphony: its governance chooses the code the press runs, and can add restrictions, censorship included. It cannot do so silently, and anyone who objects can fork the protocol and run another implementation. Polyphony makes the conditions of publishing neutral and public. It does not promise a world without censorship.

Go back, then, to the three cases.

The journalist who was let go would still be in a dispute with the site's owner, and the protocol would not decide who was right. But his years of investigations would no longer depend on the owner's goodwill. He could end his agreements and have the site rebuilt without them himself, and he would be free to publish them elsewhere. No one but him could change a word or a byline.

The writers whose platform began carrying writing they could not stand beside would not be on someone else's platform at all. The name and address their readers know would be their own. They might still disagree about what to do. But if they split, each half would keep its own work, and either could form a publication of its own.

The editor gathering the widest range of views on the war, or the law, could get the careful writers to say yes. They would not need to know in advance who else would join, because if a later piece became unbearable, they could withdraw their own piece themselves, without asking the editor and without going public.

In each case, disagreement has somewhere to go. A publication that wants to keep its writers has to persuade them, and the writers who stay are there by choice. That is what polyphony means: many voices under one name, held together by argument rather than by ownership.
