# Glenn Greenwald — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is one of the few pieces of "decentralized publishing" writing I've read that is actually about journalism rather than about tokens. Most of the genre is infrastructure porn — Merkle trees, ENS, Safe modules — in search of a problem. This draft inverts it: it opens with a real, specific human wound (two journalists who shared a beat land on opposite sides of a story, and the one who owns the room wins by default) and only then starts talking about keys. That ordering is the whole argument, and it works. The prose is unusually good — the line "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot" is the thesis, stated once and then earned repeatedly.

I am a difficult audience for this. I have spent twenty-five years watching people who hold the keys claim they don't, and I built a career partly on the premise that the press's independence from power is a matter of the people holding it, not the plumbing under it. So my verdict is genuinely split, which I'll take as a sign the paper is doing something honest rather than marketing. The diagnosis of the problem is, to my mind, nearly perfect and personally familiar. The proposed cure is elegant and I believe it is technically coherent, but it solves a narrower version of the problem than the diagnosis promises, and it quietly asks me to trust two institutions it has not argued for: a set of governors with an upgrade path, and a "payment gate" that is described but unbuilt. Both deserve harder scrutiny than the draft gives them.

I would publish this as an essay. I would not, on this draft alone, hand my byline's safety to it. That distinction is the note.

## What resonates

The core diagnosis lands on me almost painfully. "A falling-out costs the writer their income, their record, and the home of their byline; it costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced." I lived that asymmetry — not as a guest leaving a host, but as a co-founder discovering that the person who controlled the publishing infrastructure had, by definition, more leverage than I did, regardless of what the masthead promised. The draft names the mechanism precisely: "the side that can afford to walk away sets the terms." That is true, it is rarely said, and it is the correct frame for the entire problem. Whoever can leave cheaply — or, worse, whoever the other side cannot afford to lose — dictates the relationship. A publication that must persuade its contributors to stay is structurally different from one that merely outlasts them.

"The line is held by position, not argument, and no one had to choose that: it follows from the price of leaving." This is the best sentence in the paper and it is not close. It correctly relocates editorial independence from the moral character of the editor to the material structure of the relationship. This is a genuinely sophisticated move and it is what separates this paper from a thousand earnest "we must protect press freedom" essays.

I also credit the paper's discipline about what it is not claiming. It does not promise immunity from the state ("courts can compel the parties to any arrangement, this one included"). It does not promise pseudonymity as anonymity ("an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes"). It does not pretend the upgrade path is a guarantee: "This is not a guarantee to lean on." That last one, in particular, is the voice of someone who has seen a "trusted" mechanism fail and has decided to be honest about the residual trust instead of hiding it. I respect that enormously, even though I'm about to spend part of this note arguing the residual trust is bigger than the draft lets on.

## Critical readings

**1. The diagnosis is broader than the cure, and the gap is where the real power lives.** The paper opens with the case of the journalist versus the platform — the "company behind it adjusts its terms a little at a time" — and with the small collective that "wants to be in a room together by Friday." But the mechanism it actually builds addresses the *publication as a set of agreements between authors and a name.* The platform and the state both disappear from the protocol's guarantee. The draft says the independence on offer is "from each other, not from the law," and that the platform case is out of scope because the paper is about the parties to a publication. Fine. But then the strongest early passages — the ones that will make a reader believe this solves *their* problem — are precisely about the platform and the state. The reader is sold the journalist-vs-Platform story and delivered the author-vs-collective story. Those are real and related, but they are not the same fight, and the paper should say so at the top rather than letting the two blur. (Inference from general principles: I have no documented position on this specific protocol, but the distinction between "escape from a platform" and "make a collective's internal power symmetric" is central to everything I have written about media ownership.)

**2. The governors are the weak point, and "not a guarantee to lean on" is doing heroic amounts of work.** The paper concedes the upgrade path means a set of governors could adopt code that cuts a publication or author off, and waves at the "real reputational cost" of doing it publicly. I have to stop here. Everything I have learned about institutional power — and about how The Intercept's own leadership behaved when it wanted a voice silenced — is that public and costly are not the same as impossible, and that people who hold a lever find reasons to pull it and vocabulary to dress it up as principle. "It cannot be done quietly: it would be a public decision carrying a real reputational cost" is a sentence only someone who has never been on the receiving end of a public, "principled," reputationally-costless-to-the-insiders act of silencing could write. The draft names the limit honestly, which I credit, but then it treats the honesty as if it were a mitigation. It is not a mitigation; it is a description of the hole. The honest implication is that this protocol relocates a meaningful fraction of the very leverage it set out to dissolve into the hands of its governors. That may be an acceptable trade — every system has a governor — but the paper should spend real words arguing *why these governors are the right people to hold it*, not one paragraph of "where the line sits is the judgment of the appointed group."

**3. The "payment gate" is the least-journalistic part, and it is also the part most likely to be built badly.** Section 5 and the abstract both gesture at an "optional gate on reading" that "is described here and not yet built." A gate on reading is a paywall, and the draft is careful to insist the source material is never sold, only the rendered page. That is a real and important distinction — you cannot leak what you cannot extract — and I think it is the paper's most underrated technical insight. But the paper also says "dividing a payment... removes the paymaster: neither party is paid from the other's hand." As a claim about money and independence, this is too tidy. The paymaster problem in journalism is not only who writes the check; it is who can *stop* the checks and who owns the reader relationship. If the publication controls the gate and the reader list, then the author, however cryptographically sovereign over the bytes, is still dependent on the publication for reach — which is most of what a byline's "income, and audience" actually is, per the paper's own opening. Separating the cash does not fully separate the dependence. This needs either an argument or an admission. (Inference: this is a structural point about media economics, not a position I have a documented take on.)

**4. The reader is asked to trust a lot, and the paper earns that trust unevenly.** The verification section is the strongest part of the protocol, and it is genuinely good: the reader needs nothing but the page, the signature is embedded in the page, the content-addressed tree means a host cannot alter without the record breaking. That is a real accomplishment and I believe it. But "independence" in the sense that matters to a journalist is not only "the page has not been altered since the author and publication agreed on it." It is also "what got published is what I actually meant to publish, and the publication did not coerce a softening." The protocol secures the *record* of the transaction; it does not and cannot secure the *terms* of the transaction. Two people can be fully in possession of their cryptographic keys and still be browbeaten into a worse edit before the signature. The paper's own metaphor — "settled by argument, because position is no longer available to either side" — assumes the argument is a fair one. Position was never the only source of leverage. Reputation, access, fear of reprisal, the threat to not renew: none of those is dissolved by a chain. This is a limit of the design that the paper never names, and given the audience, it should.

## Sharpest questions

- If the governors' upgrade path is the residual single point of control, who are they, how are they chosen, and why should a journalist — who has spent their career not trusting groups of people with levers — trust *this* group? The paper says "this paper does not set them." Is that a humility or a dodge?
- The paper's founding scenario is two journalists on opposite sides of a story. In that concrete case, trace exactly what the protocol changes about who wins. Does the guest writer now have leverage they lacked? Or does the host publication still control reach, the subscriber list, and the domain?
- "Neither party is paid from the other's hand." Then why does the publication need to exist as a party to the payment at all? If the goal is removing the paymaster, is the strongest design the one where authors are paid directly by readers and the publication's cut is an agreement on top — or is the publication's continued role in revenue precisely the dependence the abstract promised to dissolve?
- The paper says pseudonymity is "an option, not a promise" and that it "separates the identity from infrastructure, not from the people an author chooses to tell." Who can deanonymize an author, and through what mechanism? The paper is honest that it holds no legal name — but the funding path and the rendered page both leave traces. A journalist facing a hostile state needs to know the precise boundary of the protection, and I did not find a crisp statement of it.
- Verification proves the page is what was rendered. Is there any way for an author to prove that the rendered page is what they *intended* to publish, as opposed to what the publication pressured them to accept? If not, say so — it changes what "editorial line" the protocol actually guarantees.

## Suggested changes

1. **Split the diagnosis from the cure explicitly.** Add one paragraph early stating: this protocol makes power symmetric *within* a publication; it does not by itself dissolve the power of platforms over readers or of states over parties. Then honor that by either (a) cutting the platform/state framing from the opening, or (b) — better — keeping it and adding a short, honest section on "what this does not solve," so the reader's expectations match the mechanism.

2. **Spend real words on the governors.** Replace the single "where the line sits is the judgment of the appointed group" with at least a paragraph on the selection, the threshold, the audit of their decisions, and the exit if the governors themselves become the problem. The paper's credibility with its most important audience hinges entirely on this paragraph.

3. **Tighten the "not a guarantee to lean on" concession into a design choice.** Either embrace the governor with an argument for why a governed-but-visible upgrade path beats an unchangeable one (and say what the paper's own view is), or present the no-upgrade-path variant as more than "glad to be its inspiration." As written, the concession reads like an apology for a design decision the paper has not actually defended.

4. **Give the payment gate at least a sketch of who owns the reader relationship.** A single sentence acknowledging that the gate's owner still controls reach, and that this is a residual dependence, would disarm my single biggest concern about Section 5.

5. **Name the coercion limit.** One paragraph admitting that the protocol secures the record of an agreement, not the fairness of the negotiation, would make the paper more trustworthy, not less — and it would stop a careful reader from over-inferring what "independence" means here.

## What could be shortened or left out

Section 6 (Protocol) is the section I would cut hardest, and I would cut it in place rather than entirely. It is the least journalistic, the most infrastructure-heavy, and the place where the paper's otherwise disciplined prose collapses into "a Merkle tree of content-addressed items carrying the updates" and "the Safe that owns it, the module that holds its records." The verification argument it depends on is already made, more cleanly, in Section 7. What Section 6 uniquely adds — the chain-of-authorization summary ("only approved code, running with the approved key, can produce something recorded on the smart contract") — is a genuinely important one-sentence idea, but it is buried at the bottom of a section that reads like a system diagram. I would compress Section 6 to roughly one paragraph of plain language: "rendering and tree-rebuilding are the only two actions the approved code can run with the protocol key, and the contract admits writes only from that key, so anything else fails somewhere along the chain." Everything else — the ENS details, the Safe, the module, the upgrade-path mechanics — belongs in an appendix or a companion technical note, not in the argument. A reader who is here for the journalism should be able to skip Section 6 entirely and lose nothing they need; a reader who is here for the protocol should have a place to find the detail. Right now both readers are forced through the same thicket.

## Sources

Grounded in Greenwald's documented positions: the founding and eventual departure from The Intercept over editorial/leadership disputes (public, widely reported, and echoed in his own writing on media power); his consistent skepticism of concentrated corporate and institutional control over the press; his advocacy for press freedom and against centralized platform censorship; his nuanced, non-promissory treatment of anonymity and pseudonymity (he writes under his legal name, defends anonymous sources and whistleblowers, but is explicit that anonymity is not the same as protection and comes with responsibilities); and his general insistence that institutional power is exercised through the people who hold levers regardless of stated intentions.

Labeled as inference (no documented position, reasoned from general principles): the specific structural analysis of the payment gate and reader-relationship dependence (point 3 and question 3); the distinction between "escape from a platform" and "symmetric power within a collective" as applied to this protocol (point 1); and the argument that verification secures the record but not the fairness of the negotiation (point 4). None of these should be attributed to Greenwald as established views; they are positions I would argue from the same underlying commitments.
