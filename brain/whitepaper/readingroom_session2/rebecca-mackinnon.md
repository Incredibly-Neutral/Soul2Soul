# Rebecca MacKinnon — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is a serious and unusually self-aware piece of writing about a genuinely hard problem: how groups of writers can associate and dissociate without one side holding the keys to the other's livelihood, reputation, and record. It is doing real intellectual work on the question I have spent my career circling — who holds power over speech, and whether the people subject to that power have any real say. The diagnosis is sharp and, in places, genuinely resonant. The architecture is honest about its own limits in a way that is rare and welcome.

But the paper under-theorizes the forces it claims to bracket, and it leaves the two questions where power actually concentrates — the law, and the governance of the protocol itself — almost entirely unspecified. The opening anecdote is about journalists, and journalists' ability to dissociate is constrained less by cryptography than by legal exposure and liability. The paper names the state once and steps around it. And "those who run it" — the people who choose how many governors the protocol has and at what threshold they act — is precisely the identity I have argued we must interrogate, and the draft declines to. As a proposal it is a credible and worth-building skeleton; as a statement about who can publish, whom it protects, and who is accountable, it is incomplete.

## What resonates

- **"The party with less to lose sets the terms."** This is the coercion problem at the heart of my argument about the "consent of the networked." A platform's terms are adjusted "a little at a time — a clause, a payout schedule, a content policy — each change too small to leave over, the sum of them an arrangement nobody agreed to." That is the most accurate short description I have read of how platform power accretes without consent. It is the strongest sentence in the paper.
- **"An editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot."** Genuine consent requires a real option of exit. The draft's insistence that departure must "cost both sides" — that a publication which wants to keep contributors "has to persuade them" — moves from coerced acquiescence toward something closer to association. This is the freedom-of-association frame, and it is right.
- **Named limits as a method.** The paper keeps saying "this is not a guarantee to lean on," "a limit of the design, named rather than hidden." That instinct — surfacing the cost, refusing the solutionist promise — is the accountability posture I have argued is missing from most of internet governance.
- **The vision of temporary, plural collectives** around topics and interests resonates with the collaborative, many-voices model of Global Voices — publishing that is built by a plurality of authors rather than by one institution speaking for them.
- The paper does not pretend the machinery itself is freedom. That restraint is worth crediting.

## Critical readings

**1. The state is bracketed, but for journalists the state is often the decisive power.** The paper says the protocol addresses "the parties to a publication ... and not the state," and that "the independence on offer is from each other, not from the law." That is honest, but it is a much bigger concession than the paper acknowledges. In my framework, three powers constrain online speech — states, private corporations, and criminal networks — and any design that only rearranges the private relationship while leaving the legal regime untouched has relocated leverage without answering the question that determines whether a writer can actually leave. For a journalist, the binding constraint on dissociating is often not who owns the CMS but who carries the defamation exposure, who holds the indemnification, whose libel insurance covers the byline, who answers to a subpoena, and who can be compelled to reveal a source. If the protocol relocates the publication to a pseudonymous set of machines and public addresses, it has not answered — it has arguably sharpened — the question of which party is the "publisher" in law. A hostile state, or a determined plaintiff, will simply ask who signs, who serves, and who is liable, and the paper's silence on liability allocation is the single largest gap for the very people the opening anecdote is about.

**2. "Those who run it" is where the power lives, and the paper declines to specify it.** Governors are not involved in rendering and hold only "which actions may be called and tied to the protocol key." But choosing which actions may run, and whether to cut an author or publication off, is the definition of editorial-plus-infrastructural power. The paper says the number of governors and their threshold "are the choices of those who run it; this paper does not set them." I agree the threshold is a design choice that need not be fixed here. But "this paper does not set them" is doing two very different jobs: one legitimate (we will not preset the quorum), one not (we have not specified any mechanism by which the governed can hold the governors accountable). Reputation as the sole check — "a public decision carrying a real reputational cost" — is exactly the self-regulation argument I have been skeptical of. Reputation only disciplines where there is a community that can actually exit or remove; under pseudonymous, replaceable governance that community is undefined. The paper itself concedes "this is not a guarantee to lean on," and then does not say what it would lean on instead.

**3. The reader is the forgotten third party — and that is the same mistake the paper indicts.** "Consent of the networked" was a critique of users being subject to power without voice. Here the parties are author and publication, and the reader is present only as a consumer of rendered pages and, optionally, a payer. The mechanics are symmetric between the two signatories but asymmetric against the reader: a work can be withdrawn at any moment and "the next rendering is produced without that work," with no notice or recourse to a subscriber who paid for it; the publication's line can change and the reader has no standing to see it coming beyond re-checking the record. The paper criticizes platforms for making changes "each too small to leave over"; it does not say how a reader who disagrees with a publication's change of line is any better served. Association and dissociation are treated as rights of the two contracting parties — the reader is networked, not consenting.

**4. Pseudonymity "an option, not a promise" understates the protocol's own surveillance surface.** I agree pseudonymity is not a free-standing promise and that the paper is right to separate the identity from infrastructure, not from the people an author chooses to tell. But the protocol makes the account public, the publication's revenue address public, the grants and revocations public, and — if the money gate is built — payment on a public chain. That is a transparency feature for accountability and a deanonymization vector for a hostile state. "The trail of their decisions is not" pseudonymous. A dissident's public decision trail, linked to a public payment address, is precisely the kind of metadata map that regimes use to find and punish association. The paper names the child-pornography case as a limit; it should name this one too, with equal candor. (Position on this trade-off is partly inference from my documented concern that anonymity and pseudonymity are essential for at-risk expression, especially under authoritarian rule.)

**5. The "independent nodes" in the abstract are not the architecture in §6.** The abstract promises rendering "executed by independent nodes, so no single party can produce a valid rendering alone." §6 describes a generator reachable through a Safe and an ENS name, whose actions are keyed and recorded on a smart contract. That may be a coherent design, but the paper never says who the nodes are, who runs them, who can join or leave, or where the operational power concentrates. Decentralization claims without a specified node set are where these projects usually quietly re-centralize. Whoever can marshal the nodes, or control the Safe's modules, holds the lever the paper says no one holds.

**6. The money gate is the promise that sells the paper, and it "is not yet built."** Income independence and ownership are the two headline benefits. Ownership is well specified; income independence rests on a payment gate the paper itself marks as unbuilt, "described here and not yet built," "not yet built," optional. "Neither party is paid from the other's hand" is appealing, but payments still flow through the chain, the gate's operators, and the exchanges and fiat ramps the paper elsewhere says are out of scope. The paymaster problem is realigned, not eliminated, and the paper does not say what prevents a gate provider from becoming the new platform.

## Sharpest questions

1. Which party is the "publisher" in law when a page is served — author, publication, generator operators, or the nodes? Where does defamation, copyright, and election-law liability fall, and who indemnifies the author when she withdraws? (This determines whether the opening anecdote's journalists can actually leave.)
2. Who are "those who run it," and what mechanism — beyond reputation — lets the governed (publications and authors) replace or constrain the governors? What is the exit and voice structure for the people governed by the protocol?
3. What standing, notice, or recourse does a reader have when a work they subscribed to is withdrawn, or the publication's line changes beneath them? Is the reader a consenting party or just networked?
4. Given the public account, the public revenue address, and the public trail of decisions and payments, what prevents the protocol from becoming a surveillance map of association for a hostile state? What mitigations exist, and are they worth the transparency?
5. Who are the "independent nodes," who runs them, who pays for them, and where does their power concentrate? What happens if the node set or the Safe's modules consolidate?
6. If the money gate is the income-independence half of the pitch and it is "not yet built," what is the honest near-term value of the protocol for a working journalist — is it ownership and record only, until someone builds the gate?

## Suggested changes

- **Add a liability and legal-regime section, or at minimum a paragraph** naming where defamation, copyright, and compelled-disclosure liability sit under the protocol, and how indemnification works on withdrawal. For the journalists the opening anecdote is about, this is the difference between a paper and a tool.
- **Give the reader a defined status** — even minimal: a mechanism to verify current agreements, a way to see what changed and when, a notification surface for withdrawals, and an explicit statement of whether readers have any standing. Do not reproduce the platform asymmetry the paper criticizes.
- **Split "we don't preset the threshold" from "we don't specify accountability."** Keep the quorum open, but say who constitutes "those who run it" and how the governed could replace them — a transparency floor, not a full constitution.
- **Add the deanonymization trade-off to the named-limits list**, alongside the child-pornography case, with equal candor. The public trail is a feature and a bug; say so.
- **Reconcile the "independent nodes" claim in the Abstract with the Safe/ENS/contract architecture in §6**, and state where operational power actually concentrates and how it is checked.
- **On pseudonymity**, distinguish "the protocol does not hold your name" from "your pseudonymous activity leaves a public trail that can be linked back to you." Commit at least to naming the on-chain metadata trail as a real risk for at-risk users.

## What could be shortened or left out

The clearest cut is **Section 5 (Gates)**. Its three gates are almost entirely restatement: the first gate (who may rerender) recaps the authorization logic already given in §6 ("the caller is the author of a piece, an owner of the publication, or someone the publication has authorized"); the second gate (the deal) recaps §4's "decrypted only inside the generator, while rendering, and only while the work is under a current agreement"; and the third gate (money) recaps the Abstract and §1's optional payment model. The section's only genuinely new contribution is the framing sentence — "a gate is enforced by the machinery itself ... not by any party's decision when the moment comes" — and that is a two-sentence bridge, not a section. Folding the gate inventory into §6's two-action descriptions, and keeping the one framing line, would tighten the middle of the paper without losing any argument. (For a secondary tightening: the refrain "pseudonymity is an option it permits, not a promise it makes" appears four times — Abstract, §1, §2, §7. Keep it in the Abstract and in §2, where the account design lives; the §1 and §7 repetitions add nothing.)

One thing I would not cut, despite it reading as slightly ornamental, is the closing paragraph of §1 ("There is a future for online publishing ... insights only diversity can offer"). It is the closest the paper comes to a human vision statement, and it grounds the whole mechanism in the association value it exists to serve.
