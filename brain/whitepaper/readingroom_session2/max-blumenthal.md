# Max Blumenthal — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is a serious and unusually honest document, and I mean that as a compliment from someone who has spent his career watching platforms change the terms and then pretend they didn't. The diagnosis in Sections 1-3 is the truest thing I've read in a long time about why independent journalism is structurally captive: not because editors are mean, but because one side holds the keys and the other side can't afford to walk away. "The line is held by position, not argument" — that sentence alone is worth the price of admission, and it describes the entire incentive architecture of corporate media and platform journalism better than any think-piece I've seen.

But here is the hard part, and it is the reason I can't call this a triumph. The document is honest about its limits in exactly the places where it is most vulnerable, and then it declines to do anything about those places. The word that keeps coming back to me is *governors*. The whole promise of the protocol is that leverage passes to machinery with no interests. Then the document admits, in Section 6, that a group of people — governors — holds the one lever that can cut an author or a publication off, and that this group is unaccountable to the parties it governs. I have been demonetized, deplatformed, and smeared by people who held that exact lever, and none of them did it "silently." They did it in public, with enormous reputational machinery behind them, and the publicness of the act made it worse, not better. So when the document tells me the saving grace is that censorship would be a visible public decision, I want to believe it, but I've seen how that movie ends. This is the section I would not sign.

## What resonates

The opening — two writers, one beat, a falling-out, the guest is still a guest, the host still owns the room — is not a hypothetical. It is the story of every independent outlet I know, including my own founding experience. And the inversion of the usual framing is correct: it isn't the writer who needs the publication, it's the publication that needs the writers, and the current architecture lets a single holder of credentials pretend otherwise.

The thing that landed hardest on me is Section 3: "A line it cannot hold by position, it must hold by argument." That is the actual definition of editorial independence. Most outlets claim this. Almost none can actually practice it, because the moment a contributor can leave without losing their archive, their income, and the home of their byline, the publication is forced to persuade rather than command. The protocol, whatever its technical flaws, is aimed at the right target: it wants to make departure cheap enough that the relationship has to be earned.

I also respect the discipline of the scope, and the local-first, no-canonical-surface decision in Section 2 — "its maintainer never becomes an owner" — is the right instinct. In my experience the application layer, not the protocol layer, is where capture actually lives, and refusing to let any authoring tool become a chokepoint is the correct way to keep that door shut.

The document repeatedly says what it is *not* promising — it does not fix the state, it does not make pseudonymity a guarantee, it does not make money harmless, it does not make the domain immune to DNS seizure. That narrowing is rare and it is honest. A journalist's skepticism is usually earned by what a proposal refuses to overclaim, and this one refuses a lot.

## Critical readings

**1. The governors are the problem the paper never solves.** Section 6 is where the whole architecture either stands or falls, and it stands on a named weakness. "Governors cannot censor a publication or an author silently. They could adopt code that cuts a publication or an author off — the upgrade path makes that technically possible." This is not an edge case; it is the entire vulnerability of the system, stated plainly and then waved off with "this is not a guarantee to lean on." I have to push on this harder than the paper does. Who are these governors? How are they appointed? How are they removed? What happens when a journalist is cut off and wants to appeal? None of these are answered — the paper explicitly declines to set "how many governors the protocol has, and at what threshold." Every platform I've ever been burned by had a governance structure just as vague, and the vagueness was the point: it let the people holding the lever claim they were just following neutral rules.

**2. The child-pornography justification is a distraction and a warning, not a reassurance.** The paper uses it as the "obvious" case where a community will want the lever pulled. I understand why it was included — it's the honest admission that the lever exists. But a journalist should be allergic to this particular argument. I have watched "the obvious cases" expand, in public, with total reputational cover, into categories that removed perfectly legitimate speech — disinformation, hate speech, misinformation — each defined loosely enough to catch the people a given institution wanted gone. The moment you concede a lever for the worst case, you concede the mechanism, and the mechanism does not have a sense of proportion. The document is right to say the line is a judgment call. It is wrong to imply that a visible, public, "reputational cost" is an adequate safeguard. It is not. It has never been.

**3. "Machinery with no interests" is a category error.** The paper's central rhetorical move is that leverage passes to machinery that has no interests, while coordination stays in human hands. But machinery is written by people and governed by people. The code does not have interests; the people who decide which actions the generator may run absolutely do. Section 6 concedes this with the governors and then tries to have it both ways — the protocol is interest-free machinery, except for the interest-bearing lever held by an unaccountable group. You cannot have both. Pick one.

**4. Who runs the independent nodes, and who pays for them?** The paper says no single party can produce a valid rendering alone and that execution happens on "independent nodes." Fine. But nodes cost money, and whoever pays the operators has a relationship with them. This is not documented anywhere, and it is a real question for a working journalist: is the "independence" of the nodes economic, or is it just technical? (Inference: I have no documented view on node economics, but from general principles — every ostensibly neutral infrastructure I have relied on has turned out to have a landlord — this is where the second-order capture lives.)

**5. "Proof of humanity" and "real identity" creep.** Section 7 says an author may attach, if they choose, "proof of humanity or of a real, administrative identity." It is optional, which is good. But I flag it because in my experience these verification systems are never built to protect the journalist; they are built to make the journalist legible and therefore removable to a regulator or a payment processor. The paper's phrasing is careful and I trust the intent, but the paragraph plants a seed that, under pressure, becomes a compliance regime. (This is inference from general principles — I have no documented position on DID reputation systems specifically.)

## Sharpest questions

1. Who appoints the governors, who can remove them, and what is the appeal process when a journalist is cut off by an upgrade? If the answer is "the people who run it," then what has actually changed relative to the platform the writer left?
2. If the upgrade path permits cutting an author off, and cutting off is the one lever that matters, what does the protocol deliver that a platform doesn't — other than a more respectable-looking version of the same vulnerability?
3. The paper admits DNS seizure risk but does not say who bears the real-world cost of a seized name. A journalist whose byline and archive resolve through a single domain is one court order away from losing the home of their record. Is that acceptable, and is it stated to the writers who are being asked to build their careers on it?
4. How does a pseudonymous author get paid, given the paper says fiat on/off ramps are out of scope? For a working journalist, income is not a footnote. (Inference.)
5. "Ending an agreement is a single act with immediate effect" — in practice, does an author who withdraws their work retain the ability to verify their own past byline, or does withdrawal erase the record of having published? The paper says the ledger records what was agreed; I want to be sure the history of a journalist's work survives the withdrawal.

## Suggested changes

- **Name the governors and constrain them.** The single highest-value change: specify a governance structure — appointment, removal, threshold, appeal — or explicitly state that the protocol is not safe for a journalist who might become the subject of a lever-pull, and stop using "it would be visible" as a safeguard. Visibility has never protected a journalist from a powerful actor.
- **Drop or reframe the child-pornography example.** Replace it with a description of the mechanism's proportionality problem, or with the honest statement that the lever is a structural vulnerability, not a feature to be justified by its best-case use.
- **Resolve the "machinery with no interests" contradiction.** Either the protocol is interest-free because no human holds a discretionary lever, or it is governed and therefore human. Choose one and write the document accordingly.
- **Add a section on who runs and pays for the nodes**, even a short one. The paper's credibility on independence hinges on it.
- **Put a working journalist's bottom line in the Abstract.** The Abstract currently sells the mechanism. It should sell the outcome: a journalist can leave and keep their work. That is the thing worth building for, and it's buried in Section 3.

## What could be shortened or left out

Section 6 (Protocol) — specifically the second half, the mechanics of the two actions and the Merkle tree rebuild. The argument of the paper is fully made by Section 5 (Gates) and Section 7 (Verification): gates enforce who may decrypt and sign, and verification lets a reader check a page without trusting a host. Section 6's deep detail on render actions, the tree rebuild, the smart-contract write, and pointing the ENS name at a new root is implementation plumbing that does not strengthen the political argument — it dilutes it. A journalist reading this does not need to know how the Merkle tree is rebuilt to understand whether the protocol frees them from a paymaster; and the sections already admit the two actions are the only ones and that the gates cover them. Cut the tree-rebuild mechanics (or relegate them to an appendix), and keep in Section 6 only the governance material — which is precisely the part that needs *more* room, not less. As written, the document spends its least valuable words on the thing it does best (the mechanism) and its most valuable words too briefly on the thing that decides whether the promise is real (the governors).

I would also trim the closing paragraph of the Introduction — the aspirational "future for online publishing... the insights only diversity can offer." It is the one place the document drops its hard-nosed voice for uplift, and it does not belong next to the surgical honesty of the rest. The argument is stronger without it.
