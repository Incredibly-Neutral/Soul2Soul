# Vitalik Buterin — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict
This is a genuinely interesting piece of protocol design, and unusually disciplined about its own scope. The core move — a publication as the *set of agreements currently in force* on a public chain, rather than a server or a holder — is the right mental model, and the paper mostly has the courage to follow it through to its consequences (ending an agreement is "a fact rather than a request"; the publication cannot rewrite a work, only decline to carry it). The refusal to over-claim is the strongest thing here: no payment model imposed, pseudonymity "an option it permits, not a promise it makes," independence "from each other, not from the law," the upgrade path "not a guarantee to lean on." That narrowness is exactly what I would want from a protocol spec, and it reads like someone who has internalized the discipline of not overloading a consensus system.

My reservations cluster around three things: (1) a real tension between the opening claim that "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone" and the later admission that governors *can* adopt code that cuts a publication or author off — this looks, on first read, like the "someone holds the keys" problem smuggled back in at the top of the stack; (2) the trusted computing base of the generator — where the key lives and what stops a node running modified code from extracting it is the load-bearing assumption of the whole design and gets surprisingly little scrutiny; (3) the governance layer is underspecified relative to the weight it carries. I would want those addressed before I'd call the threat model honest. Verdict: strong draft, worth engaging seriously, not yet a closed design.

## What resonates
- **Publication = set of agreements in force.** This is the right abstraction and I think it's the paper's genuine contribution. There is no separate list of articles; a work is published *because* an agreement covers it. Clean, and it makes withdrawal and versioning fall out naturally rather than being bolted on.
- **The discipline of narrowness.** Not imposing a payment model, treating pseudonymity as permitted-not-promised, keeping the state out of scope ("independence is from each other, not from the law"). This maps closely to my own repeated argument that we should not overload a protocol with things it doesn't need to do — that the smallest sound design is the more robust one. The paper explicitly says "what goes is the leverage, which was never the craft," and I respect that.
- **Ending an agreement is a fact, not a request.** Non-custodial exit with a public record of both parties' actions is the kind of property I care about. "Neither can act unseen" — the author's revisions and the publication's refusals are both recorded — is a genuinely good design goal.
- **Separation of "who governs" from "what the protocol may do."** Keeping "who governs the protocol" and "what the protocol may do" as different questions is the correct instinct, and it's the same instinct behind my push to distinguish the cryptoeconomic/social layer responsibilities in Ethereum.
- **The self-aware limit paragraph** — naming the upgrade path, admitting it can censor, and being honest that "that is a limit of the design, named rather than hidden." I much prefer a protocol that names its failure modes to one that hides them behind marketing.

## Critical readings
- **The governors re-open the central wound.** The introduction's whole promise is "no single party — author, publication, operator — can produce a valid rendering alone." Then Section 6 concedes that governors "could adopt code that cuts a publication or an author off — the upgrade path makes that technically possible." So there *is* a party that can unilaterally (at threshold) silence a publication or author. That is not equivalent to the render path, but it is a form of "someone holds the keys" — the keys to the *domain and record*, which for the reader is the whole ballgame. The paper papers over this by saying the code is visible and carries "a real reputational cost," but reputation as a deterrent is exactly the mechanism I have repeatedly warned is fragile and cannot be leaned on — the paper itself admits this ("This is not a guarantee to lean on") and then kind of leans on it anyway. I would want the paper to state plainly: *the protocol does not guarantee that the collective's governors cannot cut you off; it guarantees only that they cannot do it quietly.* Those are very different guarantees, and the abstract's first sentence undersells the distinction.
- **The generator's trusted computing base is the unexamined keystone.** The whole "neither party can produce a valid rendering alone" and the entire reading gate depend on source material being decryptable "only inside the generator," and on the generator being "open code bound to a key, executed by independent nodes." But *where* does that decryption physically happen, and what stops a node that holds (or observes) the key from running modified code or exfiltrating the plaintext or the key? If the answer is "the generator is open code so you can audit it," that answers *what* runs, not *who* guarantees the key isn't copied. If the answer is some form of trusted execution / MPC, the paper should say so, because that is the actual trust assumption of the design and it is a strong one. As written, the "no single party can render alone" claim rests on a mechanism that is asserted rather than specified. (Inference, not a documented position: but the general principle — a protocol's security claim is only as good as its weakest and least-examined trust assumption — is one I have defended for years.)
- **Pseudonymity here is honest but thin for the stated use case.** The paper is admirably explicit that the pseudonym is findable, not hidden, and that the protocol "does not hold the name behind it." Fine. But the motivating scene is journalists sharing legal exposure and wanting to dissociate from a falling-out — and for people whose *life or livelihood* depends on the name not being linked, a public, findable pseudonym with a permanent on-chain trail of decisions is close to a worst-case scenario. I'd want the paper to be clear about what it is *not* providing to that class of user, rather than implying the option covers the scenario that motivates the whole document. I have written at length that pseudonymity and privacy are different axes; this paper collapses them at moments. (My documented position — see the Decentralized Society essay and my privacy writing — is that pseudonymity ≠ privacy, and that a findable pseudonym can be worse than no pseudonym for a threatened party.)
- **The reputation / proof-of-humanity apparatus is under-justified and slightly out of place.** The last paragraph introduces "verified reputation — and, if the author chooses, proof of humanity or of a real, administrative identity" almost as an afterthought. This is SBT-adjacent territory, which I care about a lot, but here it appears to be bolted on without a problem statement. Who consumes this reputation? What attack does it prevent (sockpuppet review votes? spam?)? And given the paper's earlier honesty that pseudonymity is findable, attaching a *proof of humanity* to a findable account is a real privacy tension that isn't addressed. If the reputation layer isn't load-bearing, the paper should either give it a job or cut it. (My documented position: I'm cautiously supportive of proof-of-humanity as a concept but have repeatedly flagged the centralization and privacy risks of specific implementations, and I would not want it advertised in a protocol that elsewhere promises not to expose the name.)
- **"Resistance to censorship" is doing too much work.** It appears in the governors' survival criteria ("continuation, resilience, and resistance to censorship") alongside the admission that governors can censor a publication and that DNS can be seized/poisoned. So what *is* the censorship threat model? If the answer is "the state," the protocol does not actually resist it at the name/DNS layer and the upgrade path gives the collective a lever. If the answer is "a host," then say that and stop invoking the grander phrase. I want the paper to define resistance-to-censorship against a named adversary, or drop the phrase.

## Sharpest questions
1. **Who are the governors, exactly, and how are they chosen?** The paper says "the choices of those who run it; this paper does not set them." But the upgrade path is the single most consequential power in the system — it can cut off a publication or author. A protocol that refuses to specify who holds that power and how they're replaced is deferring the hardest question to "those who run it," which is where the trust and the attack surface actually concentrate. Is this a multisig? A token vote (which I've argued against as a governance model)? An appointee set? I'd want at least a named default and a discussion of exit.
2. **Where does decryption physically happen, and what stops the key from leaking?** Everything — the "no single party renders alone" claim, the paid gate, the "name does not vouch for a circulating copy" argument — rests on this. If the answer is trusted hardware, state the assumption; if MPC, show the threshold; if "it's open source," explain why open source prevents exfiltration of a secret (it doesn't).
3. **What happens when a hostile governor upgrade lands — even visibly?** The paper's answer is "reputational cost." But between "the code that does it is visible" and "a publication loses its name and record," the intermediate steps are unspecified. Is there a timelock? A fork/exit path for the affected parties? A way for authors to redeploy their content under a new name? Dissociation is the paper's whole subject; it should specify the dissociation *from the protocol itself*.
4. **What chain is this on, and is the "neither party operates the record" claim contingent on it?** ENS, DIDs, a Safe — this is Ethereum-shaped. But if the agreement set is on a chain, then the publication's "independence" is downstream of that chain's own governance and upgrade path. The paper argues the record is operated by "neither party," which is true only if you don't count the chain's own maintainers. I'd want that clarified, since it is the same move I warn about when people treat a layer as neutral.
5. **Can a reader actually verify, end-to-end, from a plain browser?** Section 7's "using nothing but the page" is a strong claim. A non-technical reader can't walk a Merkle path or check a DID signature. "A reader, a plugin, or an auditor" quietly smuggles in the plugin/auditor as the real verifier for most people — which is fine, but then the honest statement is "verification is available, and most readers will delegate it," which is a weaker and more accurate claim.

## Suggested changes
- **State the two-tier guarantee explicitly** in the Abstract and Introduction: the protocol guarantees that (a) no author/publication/operator can render alone, and (b) the collective cannot censor *silently* — it *can* censor openly via upgrade, and that is a real, named limit. Right now the paper oscillates between "no single party holds this position" and "governors can cut you off," and a reader doesn't know which one to hold.
- **Name the generator's trusted computing base.** One paragraph specifying the actual mechanism (trusted execution, MPC, threshold key) and its failure mode. If the authors genuinely don't know yet, say "the trusted computing base of the generator is an open problem and a subject of future work" rather than asserting the property as if it were settled.
- **Give the governance layer a concrete default.** Even a provisional one ("a fixed multisig of the initial builders, upgradeable by threshold, with a 30-day timelock and a documented fork path") would make the design testable and would force the censorship question into the open where it belongs. This is the paragraph I would most want to see expanded.
- **Tighten the pseudonymity vs. privacy distinction.** Add one or two sentences acknowledging that a findable pseudonym with a permanent public decision-trail is not privacy, and that the protocol is therefore *not* designed for users whose safety depends on non-linkability. Either scope the motivating journalist story down, or add an honest caveat that the protocol protects dissociation, not anonymity.
- **Justify or cut the reputation/proof-of-humanity paragraph.** Give it a consumer and a threat model, or remove it. As written it promises a feature (reputation, humanity proof) without a problem it solves, and it creates a privacy tension the paper doesn't acknowledge.
- **Define "resistance to censorship" against a named adversary**, or drop the phrase from the survival criteria.

## What could be shortened or left out
Concrete pick: **Section 6, the opening two paragraphs (from "A publication runs on machinery it does not control" through "the records that say what the protocol may do").** The first paragraph restates the "the protocol is two things — something governed, and something with a clear path of records" split, and the second re-walks the whole chain — domain → owner → Safe → module → records — that the paper has already established in Sections 1, 3, 4, and 7. By the time the reader reaches Section 6, this is the fourth or fifth recapitulation of "open code bound to a key, neither party alone, the record is public." It can be compressed to a single sentence ("the protocol is the machinery described in Section 4, and this section addresses who governs it") and the freed space spent on the governance/TCB questions above, which are the parts that actually need the words.

Secondary, smaller cuts: the Section 2 funding paragraph ("The account needs funding only as far as protocol fees require… no fiat on-ramp or off-ramp") doesn't carry the argument — it's a scoping note that could be folded into one clause or dropped entirely. And the closing line of the Introduction ("There is a future for online publishing in which association and dissociation… are easier") is a rhetorical flourish that the Abstract's last sentence already makes; it can go without losing anything. The child-pornography admission in Section 6, by contrast, should absolutely stay — it is the single most honest and therefore most trust-building passage in the paper, and cutting it to look cleaner would be a mistake.

## Sources
My documented positions, which this note draws on (no claim that the draft engages them; these ground my reading):
- Vitalik Buterin, "Don't Overload Ethereum's Consensus" (2023) — on keeping a consensus layer minimal and not letting every problem become a consensus/governance problem; the frame for my concern about the upgrade path and "resistance to censorship."
- E. Glen Weyl, Puja Ohlhaver, Vitalik Buterin, "Decentralized Society: Finding Web3's Soul" (2022) — SBTs, the soulbound-identity framing, and reputation; the background for the proof-of-humanity / verified-reputation critique and the pseudonymity-vs-privacy distinction.
- Vitalik Buterin, "Moving beyond coin voting governance" (2021) — the argument that token-vote governance is a weak model; my reason for asking who the governors are.
- Vitalik Buterin, "A Philosophy of Blockchain Validation" (2020) — on trust assumptions, verifiability, and "don't trust, verify" being a spectrum, not a binary; the frame for the verification and trusted-computing-base questions.
- Vitalik Buterin, various writings on d/acc and defensive decentralization — on the value of exit, dissociation, and defensive (not offensive) tooling, which the paper's ethos resonates with.
- Inference, clearly labeled: the specific mechanism of the generator's trusted computing base and the exact identity of the governors are not things I have documented positions on; where I reason about them, I reason from the general principle that a protocol's security is bounded by its least-examined trust assumption.
