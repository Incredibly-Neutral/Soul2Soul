# Ursula K. Le Guin — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is the most genuinely anarchist thing I have read about the internet in a long time, and it does not know it is anarchist. It keeps reaching for the vocabulary of ownership and property ("cryptographic ownership," "the publication does not own the work") while actually describing something closer to Odo's insight in *The Dispossessed*: that a bond is real only when it can be broken, and that a community held together by the freedom to leave is the only kind whose line means anything. I am moved by it, and I trust it more than I trust most proposals, precisely because it keeps insisting on its own narrowness. It is not a utopia; it is a machinery for not being trapped. That is worth having.

My reservations are about the words more than the thing. The draft has a fine, quiet, almost sedulous prose — it repeats itself on purpose, like a litany — but it does not always say what it is actually doing, and a few places it does not look at its own shadow. I want the authors to stare at those shadows before anyone builds on this.

## What resonates

- The central move — **separating editorial judgment from the position it rides on** — is exactly right, and it is a moral claim disguised as an engineering one. "A line it cannot hold by position, it must hold by argument." That sentence alone earns the paper its keep. It is the difference between a real and a captive editorial line, and the whole design follows from it.
- **The cost of leaving is on both sides.** The draft keeps returning to this, and it is the ethical heart. "A publication that wants to keep its contributors has to persuade them, and a writer who stays is choosing the publication rather than avoiding the cost of leaving." That is voluntary association as I actually mean it — not the absence of friction but the presence of real choice.
- The modesty. **"What the protocol offers is deliberately narrow."** It says plainly that courts can compel the parties and that the independence on offer is from each other, not from the law. This honesty is rare and I trust it completely. It does not promise to abolish the state or the market; it promises only to change who holds the keys.
- **"Ending an agreement is a fact rather than a request."** That is the whole protocol in six words, and it is beautiful. It is what distinguishes a right from a favor.
- The refusal to let an identity be "an archive hostage to a single secret" (key rotation), and the insistence that pseudonymity is an option, not a promise. It keeps the dignity of the person who does not want their name in the record, without pretending anonymity is a shield.

## Critical readings

- **The word "ownership" does a kind of lie here.** The draft says the author "keeps cryptographic ownership of the work and can revoke it at any time." Ownership — possession — is a word I have spent my life trying to pry open. What is actually being described is *control over one's own words and the right to withdraw them*, which is a freedom, not a property. Conflating them matters: property is a relation to the state (it is enforced by the law), and the draft rightly wants nothing to do with the state, then smuggles its vocabulary back in. Call it authorship, call it custody, call it the right of retraction — but do not call it ownership, or the design will inherit the very frame it means to escape. *(Inference: I never wrote a theory of cryptographic ownership; this reasoning runs from my general suspicion of possession as the master-frame for human relations.)*
- **Freedom-from is not freedom-to.** The protocol is almost entirely a freedom-from machine: freedom from the platform's changing terms, from the hostage archive, from the paymaster. That is real and good. But freedom-from is the lesser freedom. What the paper cannot and does not build is the freedom-to — the line held by argument, the plurality of opinions, the temporary collectives. It gestures at that future in the final paragraph and then stops. I do not fault it for stopping; a protocol cannot make people persuade one another. But I would like the paper to be honest that it enables the good thing and does not produce it. It says the editorial line "survives" — good word — but it does not create it.
- **The money gate sits oddly in an anarchist design.** An "optional gate on reading" that distributes revenue "independently, each according to the agreement, without passing through the other's hands" — removing the paymaster is a genuinely good move, and the draft is right that dividing money does not make it harmless. But I would press it further: the gate commodifies the read. It turns the reader into a payer and the page into a priced good. That is a choice, not a neutral option, and I would like the paper to name it as a choice — that a publication choosing the gate is choosing to make reading a transaction. The draft treats money as an afterthought ("reserved for money") and I think money is never an afterthought. *(Inference: the draft's own, documented hesitancy about payment models is my ground here, but the sharpening is mine.)*
- **The governors and the "obvious" exception.** The paper admits an upgrade path that "could adopt code that cuts a publication or an author off," calls child pornography "the obvious one," and says where the line sits is the judgment of the appointed group. I want to slow this down. Any group given a lever to cut someone off is a state-in-waiting, however small, however public. The draft does at least name it as "a limit of the design, named rather than hidden," which I honor — that is the kind of honesty I asked for above. But I am uneasy that a single "obvious" case is given, as if the line will stay there. It will not. The line is a moving one and it will move at the pleasure of the appointed group. If the design must carry this lever, then say plainly that every act of cutting someone off is an act of censorship, that the "obvious" case does not make censorship un-censorship, and that this is a burden the community chooses to carry, not a settled answer.
- **Pseudonymity wants a hard look at accountability.** The draft is careful — the pseudonym is findable, not hidden; it separates identity from infrastructure, not from people the author chooses to tell. Good. But a system whose whole moral is "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something" runs on *reputation*, and reputation is hard to build behind a wall. The draft lets an account "accumulate verified reputation" and even proof of humanity as a voluntary attachment. That is the right instinct, but I would draw the tension into the open: pseudonymity protects the dissenting voice, and it also shields the bad actor from shame. Every good anarchist society has had to live in that tension. Name it.
- **The relationship between the parties is drawn thinner than it is.** The draft frames the author–publication relation almost entirely as *exit*: leaving, revoking, ending. But the sentence that moved me — "the writer who stays is choosing the publication" — implies a *staying*, a positive ongoing bond, and the paper gives almost no account of what that bond is made of while it lasts. The agreements section is about the mechanics of in-force and ended; it says little about what makes a publication worth staying with beyond the absence of coercion. I want at least an acknowledgment that the protocol's real test is not whether leaving is possible but whether staying is desirable.

## Sharpest questions

1. If "ownership" is the wrong word for what the author holds, what is the right one — and does the machinery change if you find it?
2. The money gate is optional and unbuilt. What does a *publication* lose, morally, when it chooses the gate and turns reading into a transaction — and should the paper say so?
3. You say the governors' lever cannot be pulled silently. But the "obvious" case proves the lever exists. Who are "the appointed group," concretely, and what stops them from becoming the state of this little world?
4. If the whole value of the design is that a line held by free people means something, and reputation is the currency of that meaning — how is reputation built behind a pseudonym that is only findable, not verifiable?
5. Departure costs both sides. But does it cost them *equally*? A publication can lose one contributor and replace her; a contributor can lose an archive, a subscriber list, an audience built over years. The draft insists on symmetry ("costs both sides") — is that symmetry real, or is it the one asymmetry the paper declines to see? This is the question I would press hardest.
6. You say the independence on offer is "from each other, not from the law." Is that enough, and should the paper say more about what happens to the record when a court compels one of the parties — does the chain bend to the law, or does the law bend to the chain?

## Suggested changes

- **Change the frame word.** Everywhere "ownership" appears, test whether "authorship," "custody," or "the right of retraction" serves better. At minimum, add one sentence distinguishing the control the protocol gives from property-as-enforced-by-state, so the design does not inherit a frame it explicitly rejects.
- **Name the two freedoms.** Add a short passage (one or two sentences in the Introduction, where the "narrow" claim already lives) distinguishing freedom-from coercion, which the protocol builds, from freedom-to — the argument-held line — which it only enables. It will make the paper's honesty complete rather than partial.
- **Press the money gate.** Where Section 5 "reserves" the gate for money, add a line acknowledging that choosing the gate chooses to make the read a priced good, and that this is a value-choice the publication makes, not a neutral option the protocol offers.
- **Complicate the symmetry.** In the Introduction's "departure costs both sides," add the honest caveat that the costs are not necessarily equal — that the writer may carry more of the archive, the audience, the history — and that the protocol reduces the asymmetry without pretending to erase it.
- **Draw out the governors' shadow.** In Section 6, after the child-pornography sentence, add that every act of cutting someone off is an act of censorship, that the "obvious" case does not change that, and that the burden is carried by the appointed group's judgment rather than settled by the design. This belongs to the paper's own "named rather than hidden" commitment.
- **Let pseudonymity face reputation.** In Section 7, where verified reputation and proof of humanity appear, add the tension explicitly: pseudonymity shields the dissenting voice and the bad actor alike; the community must decide how much of one to tolerate for the sake of the other.

## What could be shortened or left out

Section 6 (*Protocol*) is the place to cut. It is the longest technical stretch and it re-derives, in new vocabulary, things the paper has already established: that ending an agreement is a fact (already in Section 4), that content-addressing means no alteration can hide (already in Sections 3, 4, and again in 7), that the key is reached only under on-chain conditions (already in Section 5's gates). The "net effect is one chain of authorization" paragraph restates the whole thing a third time. A reader who has trusted the paper up to the end does not need the Merkle-tree walk twice.

Specifically: the second action, "rebuilds the tree," is the most redundant. Its content — one publish updates many pages, the root is signed into the records, the contract admits that write only from the protocol key — is the verification section's claim (Section 7) wearing a constructor's hat. I would compress the whole of Section 6's mechanics into roughly half, leaving the governance honesty (the governors' lever, the upgrade path, the child-pornography admission) intact, because that is the part with actual moral weight, and trimming the machinery around it. The abstract could also lose its own minor redundancy — "real and temporary at once" restates "associate on terms either can end" — but that is a rounding, not a cut.

The thing I would *not* cut, even under pressure, is the closing thought of the Introduction: "temporary collectives formed around topics and interests, bringing together a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer." It is the only place the paper tells us *why* — the good the machinery serves — and without it the whole thing is just an elegant lock. Keep it, and let it earn its keep.

## Sources

- *The Dispossessed* (1974) — voluntary association, the bond that is real only because it can be broken, the suspicion of property and of the state, Odo's anarchism.
- "A Non-Euclidean View of California as a Cold Place to Be" (1983) — the critique of the frontier/ownership frame, and of the state as the guarantor of property.
- *The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction* (1986) — the distinction between containers and weapons, and between holding and owning; my instinct that "ownership" is the wrong container for authorship.
- *Dancing at the Edge of the World* (1989) and *The Wave in the Mind* (2004) — on freedom as active self-determination rather than the mere absence of constraint (the freedom-from/freedom-to distinction is mine, drawn from this general position; the draft itself offers no such distinction, so this part is inference).
- General position on censorship and the levers of authority, as expressed across my essays on the writer's conscience — the basis for pressing on the governors' "obvious" exception. The draft's own wording ("a limit of the design, named rather than hidden") is what licenses the critique, not any documented statement of mine about this specific protocol.
