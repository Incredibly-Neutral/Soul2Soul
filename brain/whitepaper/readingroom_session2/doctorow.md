# Cory Doctorow — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is the rare whitepaper that has the diagnosis right and the prescription backwards. The opening — that publishing runs on dependency, that the party who can afford to walk away sets the terms, that a thousand small terms changes add up to "an arrangement nobody agreed to" — is the enshittification argument stated better than most people state it, and it deserves to be taken seriously. The authors have clearly thought hard about the problem, and the paper is refreshingly honest about its own limits (the Section 6 passage on the upgrade path and child pornography is candor you almost never see in this genre).

But the solution is machinery where the problem is power. Every mechanism this paper builds — the chain neither party operates, the generator bound to a key, the encrypted source that only the generator may decrypt — relocates the chokepoint instead of dissolving it, and the paper's own Section 6 admits as much. My career has been one long argument that you don't fix a power asymmetry by building a box that claims to be neutral; you fix it by making exit cheap and by giving the weaker party the right to interoperate against the stronger one. This protocol gives the author back the work but leaves the audience chained to the domain, wraps the whole thing in DRM that makes readers trust the generator they can't run, and never once names the adversary it actually protects against.

It is a well-made machine. It is the wrong machine. Verdict: promising diagnosis, and a design worth arguing with — but the abstract oversells what the design delivers.

## What resonates

- **The dependency thesis.** "The side that can afford to walk away sets the terms" is my switching-costs argument in one line. Section 1's "the sum of them an arrangement nobody agreed to" is enshittification-by-increment, exactly the mechanism I describe: a platform degrades slowly, each change below the threshold of leaving, until you're living under terms you never signed. The paper understands the disease.
- **"Argument, not position."** "A publication that speaks with one voice and can point to no instance of having enforced it" and "a line it cannot hold by position, it must hold by argument" — this is the best thing in the draft. It names the soft-power mechanism by which platforms and mastheads control without ordering. That is a real insight, and it's the right target.
- **Local-first authoring and replaceable keys.** Section 2's "an identity that cannot survive a key rotation is an archive hostage to a single secret" is exactly the right instinct: don't let your record live and die in one place or one secret. This is the self-possession ethos I keep arguing for.
- **No paymaster.** "Dividing a payment does not make money harmless, but it removes the paymaster: neither party is paid from the other's hand" (Section 1) and the Section 5 description of revenue distributed independently. This is the right instinct from my chokepoint-capitalism work: the problem with the creative economy is the middleman with the funnel, not money itself.
- **Named limits.** Section 6 saying the upgrade path makes censorship *possible* but visible, that "this is not a guarantee to lean on," and naming child pornography as the obvious case — this is honest. Most protocols bury this. Respect.
- **Pseudonymity as "option, not promise."** The discipline of refusing to overpromise anonymity (Sections 1 and 2, and the "no party the protocol touches is in a position to expose one" line) is the right posture. Anonymity is hard; claiming it as a guarantee is how you lose it.

## Critical readings

- **The machine can't be neutral, and Section 6 proves it.** The abstract's headline is "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone." Then Section 6 concedes that the governors can adopt code that cuts a publication or author off — the upgrade path makes it "technically possible." So there *is* a party that can produce a valid rendering alone: the governors, acting at their threshold. The headline claim is only true ceteris paribus, and the ceteris is exactly where the power lives. This is the oldest lesson in the book: power doesn't disappear when you encode it; it moves to whoever can change the code. I have argued for years that blockchains don't resolve power asymmetries — they relocate them and add a priesthood to operate the oracle. This paper is the argument wearing a nicer suit. The honest version of the claim is "the protocol reduces the number of parties who can act unilaterally, and makes the residual power visible," which is real and worth having — but then say that, and stop selling "no single party."
- **This is DRM, and DRM is a bad deal.** Section 4: source material "stored encrypted and never made public... it exists in readable form only inside the generator." The reader receives the rendered page, never the source. That is a technological protection measure by any other name, and I've spent decades explaining what those do: they don't stop the determined copyist (Section 4 even admits a withdrawn work "may survive in copies elsewhere"), they *do* stop interoperability and they make legitimate users dependent on the vendor's machinery. Here the "vendor" is the generator — open code, yes, but code the reader does not run, executed on nodes the reader does not control. So the reader's verification ("what you see is what was verified," Section 7) is verification of a faithful render of a thing the reader cannot see. You cannot audit the source; you can only audit the generator's claim about the source. That is a trust relationship wearing a math costume. The design choice exists to make the render gate-able; the cost is that the reader is back to trusting someone else's computer, which is precisely the dependency the paper claims to have dissolved.
- **The audience is still hostage to the domain.** Section 3 makes the domain the reader-facing name: "to a reader, the publication is a domain name that authenticates the content it serves." The abstract names "the writer's byline, income, and audience" as what's held hostage, but this protocol only returns the byline and the income. The audience follows the domain, and the domain belongs to the publication. When an author leaves, their work leaves with them and the domain stops vouching for it — but every reader who cared is still standing in the old room looking at the old door. Where is the RSS move? The portable feed, the way a reader follows the *author's account* rather than the publication's domain? The author's identity is portable (Section 2, the account is what agreements record); the reader's subscription is not. My whole argument about interoperability is that the thing platforms actually hold hostage is the *relationship* with the audience, and the fix is a protocol that lets the audience follow the creator across hosts — RSS did this for a decade. This paper rebuilds the dependency at the reader layer and calls it independence.
- **"Departure costs both sides" is only true for authors with their own pull.** Section 3's lovely line — "a publication that wants to keep them must persuade them" — assumes the author can actually leave. A mid-list author with no independent reach and no portable audience leaves into silence; the cost of leaving is still ruinous for them, and the publication still only loses "one contributor, who can be replaced" (Section 1). The protocol changes the formal ownership of the work, not the economic reality of the audience. The asymmetry the paper diagnoses in Section 1 survives its own fix for everyone except the few writers big enough to walk. I'd want the paper to say that plainly instead of letting "costs both sides" do the work.
- **The residual trust set is unenumerated.** Section 7 says "at no point is the reader asked to trust whoever happens to be serving the page." But the reader trusts: (a) the generator execution environment and its operators, (b) the protocol's governors and their upgrade path (Section 6), (c) ENS governance — a registry with its own governance, its own potential for capture, a chokepoint wearing a decentralized hat — and (d) the DNS path the paper itself flags in Section 3. Enumerate the trust set honestly, then make the modest claim. The paper does this in fragments (Section 6 is honest; Section 3 is honest about DNS) but the abstract and Section 7 outrun the honesty.
- **Who is the adversary?** The paper never states a threat model. Courts can compel the parties — the paper admits this in Section 1 — so the independence is from each other, not from the law, and not from the state. Fine. Then against *what* is the machinery needed? If the threat is "the platform seizes the archive," a signed, content-addressed tree on ordinary storage plus the author's keys already answers it — you don't need a chain for that. If the threat is "one party can unilaterally rewrite the record," the chain answers it. If the threat is "the publication copies my source," the encryption answers it. But each answer imports a new trust relationship (the chain, the generator, the governors, ENS), and the paper never weighs those against what a dramatically simpler system — author-signed pages, content addressing, a domain, a git- or ipfs-style tree — already delivers for free. The marginal value of the chain over the simple system is the one number this paper owes and never provides.

## Sharpest questions

1. **Under the upgrade path, in what sense is "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone" true?** Section 6's governors can change the code and the key. So the abstract's central promise is exactly what the design reserves the power to undo. Which is it: a property of the system, or a preference of its operators? If the latter, say so in the abstract.
2. **Where does the audience go when the author leaves?** The work is portable, the author's account is portable, the reader's subscription is not. Is there any feed-level portability (the RSS move), or does departure still mean rebuilding an audience from zero? If the latter, "departure costs both sides" is false for everyone below a celebrity threshold — and the paper's own diagnosis in Section 1 says that threshold is where the power sits.
3. **Why should a reader trust the generator more than a platform?** The reader does not run the generator, does not audit the source, and receives a render produced by nodes they don't control (Sections 4, 7). What, concretely, is different in the reader's experience between "a platform renders my page" and "independent nodes render my page," beyond which particular third parties happen to be in the room this quarter?
4. **What is the smallest viable read?** Section 5's gate pays per read or by subscription, and the read is gated on-chain ("the record shows that reader has paid"). Per-read on-chain accounting has a transaction-cost floor. What is the minimum price at which per-read works? If the floor is above what a casual reader will pay, the design quietly becomes subscription-only — fine, but say it.
5. **Where does the money to run the protocol come from, and from whom?** Section 2 says the account needs funding "only as far as protocol fees require" and that no fiat on-ramp is required. But gas, publishing, and rendering are on-chain writes with a cost. A pseudonymous author still needs crypto to pay gas, which *is* an on-ramp in practice, and it is a regressive one — [inference from general principle; I have no documented Doctorow position on gas specifically] the people who most need independence from a platform are often the ones who can least afford to maintain a funded, gas-paying account in perpetuity.
6. **What does the reader actually verify?** Section 7: "what you see is what was verified." Verified against what — the source, which the reader cannot see? If verification is only of render-fidelity, then the proof the page "has not been altered" is a proof about bytes the reader never inspects. Can a reader, today, check the source of a page against its claimed provenance, or is that privilege reserved to the generator?
7. **Who governs ENS, and is that a chokepoint?** The name is the publication (Section 3, 7), and names resolve through ENS. ENS has its own governance and its own upgrade-like paths. Is "the name can be seized" a solved problem, or has the paper just moved the seizure point one layer up from DNS?

## Suggested changes

1. **Move Section 6's honesty into the abstract, and fix the headline.** Rewrite "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone" to state the residual power set up front: the governors via the upgrade path, ENS governance, the DNS path, the reader's trust in the generator execution environment. The paper's credibility is in Section 6; put it on the front door. (Sections Abstract + 6.)
2. **Add the reader-portability layer — the RSS move.** Make subscriptions attach to the author's account, not only to the publication's domain, so a reader who follows an author keeps following them after departure. This is the single change that would make "the audience" actually free, and it is the change my whole adversarial-interoperability argument says is the real cure. Without it, the protocol frees the work but not the reader, and "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something" stays conditional on the author already having leverage. (Section 3, and the abstract's "audience.")
3. **Reconsider the source-DRM.** State the specific harm the source encryption defends against, and weigh it against the verification loss and the reader's dependence on the generator. If the harm is "the publication copies the source," that is a real but narrow threat; if it is anything broader, the encryption is doing DRM's work and will earn DRM's costs. Alternative worth exploring: author-signed, content-addressed plaintext source with the render being a *derivation* that any reader can re-derive and check — verification without the generator as an oracle. (Sections 4, 7.)
4. **Price the smallest read, or admit the floor.** State the minimum viable per-read price given on-chain accounting, or say plainly that per-read only works above a transaction-cost floor and that subscription is the default. Amortizing fees across subscriptions is the obvious answer; say so. (Section 5.)
5. **Enumerate the trust set.** Replace the "no trust in the server" framing of Section 7 with an explicit, numbered list of what the reader still trusts (generator operators, governors, ENS, DNS). Then the claims can be as strong as the list allows. (Section 7.)
6. **Add a one-paragraph threat model to the Introduction.** Name the adversary the protocol defends against (each other, not the state, not the law — the paper already says this) and what it does *not* defend against (governors by upgrade, ENS capture, courts). This forces the marginal-value question: what does the chain add over signed pages plus content addressing plus a domain? Answer it, even briefly, and the whole document gets more honest. (Section 1.)

## What could be shortened or left out

**Cut the final paragraph of Section 1 (Introduction).** "There is a future for online publishing in which association and dissociation between groups of people are easier and involve less friction: temporary collectives formed around topics and interests, bringing together a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer." This is the one paragraph in the draft that is pure sentiment — no mechanism, no argument, no claim the paper's machinery substantiates, and no connection to anything Section 2–7 actually builds. It reads as uplift bolted onto a technical document, and worse, it commits the exact sin the paper elsewhere condemns: it is *position*, not argument. The paper's own thesis is that a line "held by argument, not position" is the only one that means anything; ending the introduction on an appeal to "plurality" and "diversity" and "insights" is a disposition wearing a conclusion's clothes. Cut it and the argument loses nothing — the document closes stronger on the Section 1 line about an editorial line held by people free to leave. (If a nod to the collective vision is wanted, one clause in the abstract already gestures at "temporary collectives" and can carry it.)

And one housekeeping cut: delete the stray task checkbox at the top of the file, "- [ ] buy groceries," sitting between the frontmatter and the Abstract. An artifact like that in a canonical draft is exactly the kind of "no one had to choose that, it just follows" detail the paper's own argument says corrodes trust. Groceries: out.
