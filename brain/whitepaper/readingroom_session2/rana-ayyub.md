# Rana Ayyub — reading-room note (first read)

## Verdict

This is a genuinely interesting piece of engineering-adjacent political thinking, and it is written with unusual care — the prose is tight, the honesty is real (it names its own limits), and the central move is sound: separate editorial judgment from the position it currently rides on. I read it as a journalist who has been hounded not by a publisher but by a state, and from that seat I keep circling one thing the paper is honest about on page one and then, I think, underweights everywhere else: **the threat this protocol actually removes is horizontal, between writer and publication — not vertical, between the writer and the state.** The draft says this plainly in Section 1 ("the independence on offer is from each other, not from the law"), so it is not hiding anything. But for someone like me, that admission is not a footnote; it is the whole question, and I want the paper to feel its weight.

A strong, disciplined draft. It knows exactly what it is and what it refuses to be. It just does not — I think — know how little of my own worst-case it is built to survive, and it should say more about that, or its own claimed honesty is incomplete.

## What resonates

- **"The party with less to lose sets the terms."** This is the truest sentence in the document, and it is not an abstraction to me. When you publish work that a government wants buried, the thing that keeps you going is not the platform — it is the reader who stays because you told them a hard thing. The insight that a line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot is exactly right, and it is the emotional and political core of the paper. It is why I kept reading.
- **"What accumulates is not a policy but a disposition."** This names something every editor and reporter already knows: censorship rarely arrives as a single order; it arrives as a thousand small adjustments nobody agreed to, until the arrangement is unrecognizable. The paper's insistence that *no change is silent* and that every decision is "an act, not a setting" is a direct and honest answer to that slow drift. I believe this is the correct instinct.
- **The refusal to impose a payment model, and the separation of "who pays" from "who can cut you off."** As someone whose survival has at times depended directly on readers choosing to fund work the establishment would rather starve, I find the "no paymaster — neither party is paid from the other's hand" framing genuinely good. It gets at something structural, not just technical.
- **Pseudonymity as "an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes."** This is the right, disciplined thing to say. Too many crypto projects oversell anonymity. This one refuses to, and I respect the refusal.

## Critical readings

- **The state is the blank in the room.** My documented experience is not a hostile platform; it is sedition-style investigations, tax raids, surveillance, and a campaign to make publishing the truth unaffordable and dangerous. The paper names the court and then walks past it: courts can compel the parties, so independence is "from each other, not from the law." But compulsion is only the crude end. The sophisticated end is what a state does to a journalist's *ability to exist* — and none of that touches whether the writer can revoke a signature or the publication can end an agreement. I am not saying the protocol should have solved state power. I am saying the paper currently presents its honest limit in one clause and then builds forty paragraphs as though the author-versus-publication axis were the whole problem. It is not the whole problem, and a reader with my biography will feel the gap on every page.
- **Where are the sources?** My career runs on confidential informants. The paper encrypts "source material" and keeps it readable only inside the generator — but what it means by "source material" is the *manuscript*, the pre-publication work, not the informant. The person who gave me the document, the identity of the whistleblower, the trail of how I obtained what I obtained — none of that is in this model, and the paper never says it is out of scope. It is not even acknowledged. For a journalist, that is the load-bearing secret. I want either a real treatment of it or an explicit, honest "this protocol does not touch your research and communications trail" — which is a fine and even desirable thing to say, but it should be said.
- **Reputation on a fresh pseudonym.** Section 7 says an author's account "can accumulate verified reputation," and Section 2 says the identity is "permanent enough to carry a reputation." But a famous byline is not just a signature that persists; it is accumulated trust that took years and a body of verified risk to earn. How does a pseudonymous account, findable but with nothing tying it to a person, build the byline-weight that made my name mean something? The paper hand-waves "verified reputation" without confronting that a pseudonymous account that can be *proven* to be someone is no longer a pseudonym, and one that cannot be proven can be impersonated, hedged, or disavowed. There is a real tension here and I do not think the draft has thought it through.
- **The child-pornography lever.** Section 6 is brave to name the upgrade path and to concede that "a community will want the lever pulled, and child pornography is the obvious one." But it should hear how that sentence sounds to someone who has watched the phrase "protect the children" become the door through which exactly the censorship they warned about walks. The lever exists; a community holds it; "where the line sits is the judgment of the appointed group." Appointed by whom, and in whose interests? I am not arguing the lever should be absent — the paper's honesty about the trade-off is its best quality — but it states the compromise without reckoning with how reliably such levers get widened against the very people the protocol claims to protect.

## Sharpest questions

1. If a court can compel the parties to any arrangement, and a state can make your life unaffordable and dangerous regardless of who holds the keys — **what, precisely, did this protocol buy the journalist whose enemy is the state rather than the platform?** Give me the honest, narrow answer, not the one implied by the other forty paragraphs.
2. **Where do the confidential sources sit in this model?** Is the encrypted "source material" the manuscript only? Does the protocol touch the informant's identity, the research notes, the communications trail — and if it does not, will the paper say so out loud?
3. How does a **pseudonymous account accumulate the reputation Section 7 promises** without either (a) collapsing into a de-anonymized identity or (b) being impersonable and disavowable? Which of those two failures is the design actually accepting?
4. The upgrade-path lever in Section 6 — **who is "the appointed group," and what stops the lever from being widened** from the obvious case to the merely inconvenient one? The paper names the limit; it does not bound it.
5. Since a publication's name rides on DNS and "can be seized, poisoned, or made hard to resolve," and since **whoever seizes the name can cut readers off from the content** regardless of the on-chain record — how much of the independence is real when the door readers walk through is still owned by someone else?

## Suggested changes

- **Add an explicit "what this does not do against the state" subsection** (or make Section 1's single clause a real paragraph). Not to apologize, but to complete the honesty the paper clearly values. State plainly: this protocol rearranges the relationship between writer and publication; it does not protect a journalist from a government that wants them harmed, and it does not pretend to. A critic with my biography will respect that statement far more than the current silence around it.
- **Scope the sources question explicitly.** Either a short treatment of how (or whether) the protocol relates to confidential informants and research trails, or a clear one-line "this is outside the protocol's scope." Leaving it unmentioned reads as an oversight, and for an investigative journalist it is the most important thing you did not say.
- **Revisit the reputation paragraph in Section 7.** Say, concretely, how a pseudonymous account accrues "verified reputation" without recreating a de-anonymizing authority or remaining trivially impersonable. If the answer is "it is genuinely hard and the protocol does not fully solve it," say that — it is the kind of admission that would strengthen the paper.
- **Tighten the child-pornography passage** so the concession is a bounded design statement, not an open invitation: say who holds the lever, at what threshold, and what public record makes a widening visible. You name the limit; name its edges too.

## What could be shortened or left out

**Section 6 — Protocol — is the section to trim, and specifically the two-actions detail (the "render" action and the "rebuild the tree" / Merkle-tree action).** The paper's argument does not need the mechanism: that a change to one page forces changes to others, that patterns are declared in the publication's config, that the key "signs the tree's root into the records on the smart contract, pointing the ENS name at the new root." What the reader actually needs — and what the section's own closing paragraph delivers perfectly — is the **"one chain of authorization"** summary: only approved code, running with the approved key, can produce something recorded and served. The Merkle-tree mechanics are implementation detail that slows the section and, worse, gives the critic of the power balance a haystack to hide the real argument in. I would compress the whole of that mechanism into two or three sentences (or move it to an appendix) and let Section 6 spend its remaining space on the things that matter to me: who governs, what the lever can do, and where the line sits. The same instinct applies, more mildly, to the Abstract — it is long and Section 1 restates it almost point for point; the Abstract could lose a sentence or two without losing the argument.

## Sources

Grounded in Rana Ayyub's documented positions: her investigative reporting on the Gujarat riots and the state's role in them (*The Gujarat Files*, 2016); her account of living under surveillance, threats, and legal and financial harassment after reporting on figures around the Indian government (*It Is Not What You Think*, 2018); her extensive public writing on press freedom, the weaponization of sedition-style law, tax raids, and the online and offline campaign of abuse against her as a Muslim woman journalist; and her experience of reader-funded survival and of pseudonymity/security trade-offs under state and mob pressure.

Where the draft raises matters on which I have no documented view — the specific mechanics of Merkle-tree rendering, ENS/domain resolution, and the smart-contract structure — I have reasoned from general principles and labeled it as inference rather than presenting it as my settled position. The central concerns above (the state, sources, reputation, the censorship lever) are grounded in my documented experience, not inference.
