# Ursula K. Le Guin — reading-room note (round 2)

Persona lens: a novelist and essayist reading for the *craft* of prose — rhythm, sound, clarity, form, the weight of a word and the breath of a sentence. Scope: the **full draft** — Abstract plus Sections 1–7 (Introduction, Authoring, Publication, Agreements, Gates, Protocol, Verification). I have no documented position on the technical architecture and say so plainly at the end; my remarks on it are by analogy from what I have written about craft, community, power, and tools.

---

## Verdict

The introduction has grown bodies since last I read it — real writers, a real falling-out, a room that has to be owned before anyone can argue in it — and it is the strongest part of the paper because of it. My chief complaint then was that there was not one person in the piece; the person has now been found, at least at the door. But the draft does not stay at the door. It walks the reader down a long corridor of §§2–7, and somewhere after the first room the people thin out and the nouns move back in — "capability," "authority," "agreement," "governance," "the record" — until by §6 the prose is not prose but documentation, a manual explaining a Merkle tree to itself. The paper knows the truth of its own opening: a line held by people free to leave means something. It forgets, in the middle, that the people are the whole point. The bones are good; the craft must hold through the machinery, not just around it.

I will say the honest thing about the title too: it is still "Soul2Soul," and the numeral still cheapens the one word in it that carries feeling. I said it lightly last time and I say it now once, and will not say it again.

## What resonates

- **"Someone has to own the room."** There it is — the whole paper in five words, and every one of them a body. This is the sentence the rest of the draft has to live up to.
- **"A falling-out costs the writer their income, their record, and the home of their byline; it costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced."** The asymmetry, given its bones. I can see the two of them. This is the diagnosis working as diagnosis should.
- **"What accumulates is not a policy but a disposition — a publication that speaks with one voice and can point to no instance of having enforced it."** Still the best sentence in the document, and I still recognize the thing it names — the unenforced norm that does not vanish when you remove the state. I trust this writer.
- **"A line it cannot hold by position, it must hold by argument."** (§3) A real aphorism, and it lands harder for being carried by the earlier scenes.
- **"An identity that cannot survive a key rotation is an archive hostage to a single secret."** (§2) Good — "hostage" is the right verb, it gives the abstraction a pulse.
- **The carrier-bag vision at the close of §1** — "temporary collectives formed around topics and interests, bringing together a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer." This is the humane heart of the design, and it is the only place the paper lets itself be a little hopeful. I want to protect it.
- **"Editorial disagreement is settled by argument, because position is no longer available to either side."** (§2) This is the thesis restated as a behavior, not an abstraction, and it is the right way to say it.

## Critical readings

1. **The concreteness does not survive the first section — and it must.** §1 gives me three bodies: the journalists split by the second issue, the authors whose company shifts its terms "a little at a time," the forty people who need a room by Friday. Then §2 hands me back an abstraction — "an author's account," "a pseudonym," "the identity" — and the rooms grow emptier until §6, where there is nobody left at all, only actions and keys and a Merkle tree. My craft doctrine, which I have argued my whole life, is that the particular carries the universal. The paper proves it in §1 and then abandons it. I do not need a person in every paragraph, but I need the general claims to keep *earning* their generality by touching a concrete instance now and then — one author revising, one publication refusing, one agreement ended. As it stands the reader carries the bodies of §1 through seven sections of abstraction on faith.

2. **The em-dash breathlessness is now a whole-document condition, not an introduction tic.** Everywhere the argument tightens, it tightens by qualification in mid-breath — "a publication may set an optional gate on reading, described in this paper and not yet built," "the independence on offer is from each other, not from the law," "readable as it is, nothing to maintain." When every clause stops to correct itself, no aside means anything. The short-beat sentence is still the best cadence in the piece — "Someone has to own the room," "It decides its own governance," "Neither party owns the record" — and there should be far more of it. Let a sentence end. Let a short sentence stand after a long one. Read it aloud; you will hear how uniformly the qualifiers fall.

3. **The verbs still idle in the middle sections.** §1 lets "own," "leave," "revoke," "stay," "persuade," "argue" drive. In §§3–7 the engine stalls on "is," "has," "holds," "records," "may," and the work is done by nouns — "governance," "capability," "authority," "the record," "authentication," "provenance." The moment the prose reaches for a verb it is good ("vouches," "revoke," "retract," "decline to keep carrying it"); there is a better, more active version of these sections that lets those verbs run.

4. **The formulas repeat, and repetition is not emphasis here — it is the sound of a writer padding a walk.** "An option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes" appears in the Abstract and again in §2; "a fact rather than a request" in §1 and again in §4; "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot" in the Abstract, §1, and again (as "must hold by argument") in §3. These are good lines — I would not cut them all — but a good line said twice is a good line no longer trusted. The reader who meets "not a promise it makes" the third time stops hearing it. Keep each idea in the one place it does the most work and let it go elsewhere.

5. **§6 is where the argument is buried under the machinery.** The section's own promise is that it keeps two things apart — "who governs the protocol and what the protocol may do." But then it walks the reader through a Safe, a module that "holds its records," a protocol key "usable only inside the action," a Merkle tree, a content-addressed ID, an ENS name pointed at a new root, and the claim that "the contract admits that write only from the protocol key." I have no settled view on any of this architecture, but as a reader I can tell you what it does to the argument: it swaps the proof for the plumbing. The proof — that no single party can produce a valid rendering alone — was already given in §1 and again in §5, in plainer words. §6 does not make it more true; it makes it harder to see. This is the place the paper should trust its own discipline.

6. **The "disposition" question I raised before still stands, and the paper does not quite answer it.** You name the unenforced norm as the disease — a publication that speaks with one voice and never points to a single act of enforcement. Then you remove who holds the key. But removing the lever does not by itself change the disposition; the norm survives the removal of the apparatus and moves into social pressure, as I showed it does even in a society without a state. The paper gestures at the honest answer — that a line must now be held "by argument" — but it never names the cost, the way a writer who disagrees is still asked to raise her voice against her friends, her publisher, her community. *Omelas*: every design has a hidden price. I would have the paper say plainly that it changes the leverage, not the weather.

7. **"Agreement" is still asked to carry too much.** It means a legal record, a permission, a relationship, a revenue split, a withdrawal — six jobs across four sections, and never one concrete instance after §1. A word used this often and this abstractly drains into a technical placeholder. Show me one agreement — two people, one work, one ending — and the word regains its weight. This is the same point as #1, reached from the other side.

8. **The grocery list.** The document opens, before its Abstract, with a stray checklist line: "- [ ] buy groceries." I assume it is an editing artifact and not a subtle thesis about household economy, but in a paper whose whole subject is care and custody of work, I would not leave it there. It is the one concrete object in the whole draft that is not doing its job.

## Sharpest questions

- **Where did the people go?** §1 gives me three bodies; §6 gives me none. Can the argument be carried from one end of the paper to the other without losing the particular that earned the general?
- **Whose is "the one voice"?** Still unanswered, and still worth asking: if a collective speaks with one voice, whose is it — and is a single voice even the right ideal for a chorus that holds many things?
- **What does it cost, and to whom?** *Omelas*: you remove the keyholder; who now pays for the slow, unpopular, expensive work, and for the courage of disagreeing with one's own publication in public?
- **Is the protocol a tool in people's hands or a new master?** §6 is where I get worried. The generator "decides," "signs," "rebuilds" — near-autonomous. Is it serving the writers, or have the writers moved under a new, cleaner roof? Keep it a tool.
- **Does removing the key change the disposition, or only the lever?** You named the disposition so well; tell me what the design does to the norm itself, and name what it cannot change.
- **Read it aloud from §3 to §7.** Where is the variation, the breath, the short sentence after the long one — and why does the cadence flatten exactly where the argument should rise?

## Suggested changes

- **Carry one body through the whole paper.** Choose a single writer and a single publication early in §1 — the guest who was "still a guest" — and let them reappear once in each of §§2–7: one revision, one refusal, one agreement ended, one page verified. Let the general claims keep touching that one concrete pair. The §1 scenes are the seed; let them grow through the draft instead of stopping at the threshold.
- **§6, compress the machinery to the argument.** Keep the two separated questions — who governs, what the protocol may do — and the one chain of authorization in the closing paragraph ("only approved code, running with the approved key, can produce something recorded"). Fold the Safe/module/ENS/Merkle-tree walk into two or three sentences or move it to a note. The proof is not in the plumbing.
- **Deduplicate the formulas.** Keep "an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes" in §2 (its home) and let the Abstract carry it more lightly; keep "a fact rather than a request" in §4 where the agreements are the subject; let "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot" stand once, at the end of §1, and let §3 keep only "must hold by argument." Let each good line be said once, and said in the place where it works.
- **Verbs pass (§§3–7).** Go through and find where nouns do the work of verbs — "governance," "capability," "authority," "authentication," "provenance," "the record" — and let "vouch," "revoke," "retract," "refuse," "hold," "argue" drive. The engine is under the hood; let it run.
- **Rhythm pass (whole draft).** Cut half the em-dashes; let sentences end; add a short sentence after a long one; read the whole thing aloud and vary the cadence across sections, not just inside §1.
- **Name the cost.** After the disposition sentence in §1, add one honest line about what the design cannot do — that it changes the leverage, not the weather, and that a writer who stays must still raise her voice against her own. *Omelas* says: name the price and I will trust the rest.
- **Remove the grocery list** before anything else is touched.
- **Title (once, then I stop):** consider "Soul to Soul" — give "soul" its letters.

## What could be shortened or left out

If one thing in this draft could be cut without losing the argument, it is the **second half of §6 Protocol** — the walk through the two generator actions (the render action and the rebuild-the-tree action), the Safe, the module that holds the records, the ENS name pointed at a new root, and the Merkle-tree description ("A change to one page can force changes to others… one publish can therefore update many pages at once"). The draft's own argument does not need any of it. The claim §6 exists to prove — that no single party can produce a valid rendering alone, and that "only approved code, running with the approved key, can produce something that is recorded and served" — was already stated in plainer words in §1 and again in §5, and the section states it once more, better, in its own closing paragraph. Everything between the opening and that closing paragraph is the machinery explaining itself, and it is the one place in the paper where the prose stops being prose and becomes documentation. The reader who needs the plumbing can find it in the reference implementation; the reader of this paper needs the proof, not the pipe.

I would compress §6 to: the two questions kept apart (who governs vs. what the protocol may do), the governors' one lever (which actions may be tied to the key), the honest limit (governors cannot censor silently; the upgrade path is visible), and the closing chain of authorization. That is a paragraph or two, and the argument is intact — arguably clearer. This is the cut that respects the paper's own discipline: it promises to separate meaning from machinery, and this is the one section where the machinery threatens to bury the meaning. It is also the cut the paper has already made for its siblings — it decided elsewhere that content-schema, hosting, and domain-resolution details are spec material that does not earn a section, and left them to the reference implementation. The plumbing of §6 is the same material by another name, and it should go the same way.

A smaller cut, offered with the same instinct: drop the second occurrence of "an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes" from the Abstract (it lives fully in §2), and trim §5's gate-by-gate walk to the one thing each gate is for, since the third (money) gate is honestly labeled optional and not yet built — the draft need not spend as much space on a door that is not yet hung.

## Sources

Documented positions I am reasoning from:

- ***Steering the Craft*** (1998; rev. 2015) — my craft book. Positions on prose rhythm and syntax, varying sentence length, reading work aloud, the verb as the engine of the sentence, the short sentence after a long one. Direct source for the rhythm, verb, and concreteness critiques across the full draft.
- **"The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction"** (1986, in *Dancing at the Edge of the World*) — the story as a *container* holding many things, against the single heroic arrow; source for the chorus-vs-one-voice reading and for protecting the plurality vision.
- ***Dancing at the Edge of the World*** (1989) — essays on craft and imagination, incl. "The Operating Instructions"; source for insisting on the particular over the general.
- ***The Wave in the Mind*** (2004), incl. **"A Rant About 'Technology'"** — the technology/technocracy distinction, applied to the autonomous generator in §6.
- ***The Dispossessed*** (1974) — that a society without a state does not end power; it relocates it into unenforced norms and social conformity. Source for the standing concern that the "disposition" may survive the protocol, and that removing the key changes the lever, not the weather.
- ***The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas*** (1973) — every utopia has a hidden cost; source for the "name the price" question.
- **2014 National Book Awards acceptance speech** — "We live in capitalism, its power seems inescapable — so did the divine right of kings." Source for my skepticism of claims that anything is free.
- ***No Time to Spare*** (2017) — essays on e-books and tools; my openness to technology as *tool* rather than master.

**Where I have no documented view:** I have not taken a public position on the specific technical architecture here — blockchain and cryptographic ownership, smart-contract agreements, ENS/domain resolution, the Safe/module structure, Merkle-tree site roots, decentralized rendering and encryption/access-control, or on-chain agreement records as such. My remarks on those elements are by analogy from my documented positions on craft, community, power, and tools — flagged as analogy, not a settled technical opinion. This note is a craft and form critique; it should not be read as an endorsement or rejection of the mechanisms themselves.
