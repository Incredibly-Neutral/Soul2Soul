# Glenn Greenwald — reading-room note (round 2)

## Verdict

This is the strongest version of the draft yet, and the strongest because it finally stops trying to promise independence and starts being honest about what it can and cannot deliver. The two moves that matter — separating editorial judgment from the position it currently rides on, and confessing that the independence on offer is *from each other, not from the law* — are the heart of the whole paper, and they are said plainly. For a protocol whose founding instinct is the freedom of a writer to leave, the draft is now willing to state its own limits. That is the difference between a whitepaper and a pitch, and this reads like the former.

I would still not call it finished. The central claim — that a writer's leverage survives because the *publication* also wants to keep *them* — is asserted more than it is earned. The paper is strong on how the machinery works and weak on why, in the real world, anyone with power in this arrangement would not simply find a new lever. And the draft's eagerness to be modest ("narrow," "deliberately narrow") sometimes reads as under-arguing its own case rather than as restraint. Keep the honesty; earn the optimism.

## What resonates

- **"The line is held by position, not argument, and no one had to choose that: it follows from the price of leaving."** This is the single best sentence in the draft. It names the mechanism of a captive press better than most essays on platform power do — that coercion is not an act but an accumulated disposition nobody ever voted on. This is precisely the dynamic that has destroyed independent journalism inside platforms, and it is correctly identified as the thing to be dismantled.
- **The separation of editorial judgment from position.** "Editorial judgment survives. What goes is the leverage, which was never the craft." This is the moral core and it is correct: the craft of editing and reporting is not the same as the power to fire or withhold the byline's home. The draft is right that these have been conflated and that the protocol separates them.
- **"It addresses the parties to a publication — the writers, the name, the readers — and not the state."** This is exactly the right scope and exactly the right humility. Any press-freedom tool that pretends to protect against the state is lying to you. The protocol can't stop a subpoena or a gag order or a compelled-key seizure, and the draft does not pretend otherwise. For a reader who has actually been subpoenaed, this honesty is the thing that makes the rest of the document worth reading.
- **"Neither party is paid from the other's hand."** The paymaster problem is real and underdiscussed in the crypto-publishing space. Removing the structure in which one party can defund the other is a genuine, concrete improvement over "we run our own server."
- **The permissionless exit in §4 — "The author does not ask, and the publication does not need to agree."** A withdrawal that is a fact rather than a request is the entire point of the protocol, and it is stated without hedging.

## Critical readings

1. **The reciprocity of lock-in is asserted, not proven.** The whole design rests on the claim in §1 and §3 that "departure costs both sides" — that a publication which wants to keep its contributors must persuade them. But in journalism the asymmetry that actually exists is almost never symmetric: the publication controls the *audience*, the *archive's* discoverability, the *reputation* that took years to accumulate, and — critically — the *subscriber list / revenue stream*. A writer can take their cryptographic bytes with them; they cannot take the audience. The draft says the revenue goes to the publication's own address and the name resolves — which is precisely the admission that the *money and the readers stay with the name*. So who really has to persuade whom? The draft never confronts that the scarce asset in this arrangement is audience, not content, and that the audience is bound to the *domain*, not to the writer. Until this is engaged, the "both sides lose" claim is the paper's softest spot.

2. **The child-pornography paragraph (§6) is a trap the draft walks into voluntarily.** It is honest to name the upgrade lever and that it can be pulled. It is something else to volunteer the specific example — "child pornography is the obvious one" — and then hand the decision to "the judgment of the appointed group." A journalist should not need to be told why you do not put "the appointed group decides what is censorable" in a press-freedom document and call it a named limit. The sentence "This is not a guarantee to lean on" does not undo it. Either the protocol has no content censorship lever, or it has one and someone controls it; the draft should say which and stop decorating it with the most extreme example available. As written, it reads like it is asking the censors to justify themselves — the opposite of the paper's thesis.

3. **"Pseudonymity is an option, not a promise" is doing too much work and covering too little.** It is said four or five times, as if repetition were argument. The draft is right that the protocol cannot protect a pseudonym from the people an author chooses to tell, and it is right that the account is public. But the sentence never answers the question a source-protection reader actually has: *what is the protocol's exposure model?* If the account is pseudonymous, the record public, and source material encrypted — what precisely does a compelled-key or subpoena scenario look like? The paper is admirably honest that it is not a promise, but it stops at the honesty and never provides the threat model that would make "option, not promise" mean something concrete. Given that the abstract itself flags this, the §2 treatment is thinner than the claim deserves.

4. **DNS is a paragraph, but it may be the whole risk.** §3 concedes, in three sentences, that the name can be "seized, poisoned, or made hard to resolve" and that the protocol does not make it immune. For a document about the price of leaving, the domain is where a hostile state or a determined adversary would attack — and where the *publication's* control over the name becomes a lever over the writer again, and vice versa. This is one of the two or three most important paragraphs in the paper and it is treated as a parenthetical. If the independence claim depends on the name resolving, then the failure modes of the name are not a caveat; they are the specification.

5. **The "proof of humanity or of a real, administrative identity" in §7 is introduced, then dropped.** It is a one-line tease in the final section — an option an author "may" attach — with no mechanism, no privacy analysis, and no acknowledgment that proof-of-humanity and verified identity are precisely the features that would (a) undermine pseudonymity and (b) become the state's favorite access point. If it is in the paper, it needs a paragraph. If it is not ready, it should not be in the paper at all, because a hostile reader will take it as the design's admission that real identity is the eventual goal.

6. **Governance is "the choices of those who run it" — and that is a real person with real power.** §6 says the paper "does not set" how many governors or at what threshold, only that the choices are explicit and public. But "explicit and public" is not a check on the governors; it is a transparency feature. Some concrete entity decides continuation, resilience, and censorship-resistance. The draft never says who is plausibly *motivated* to run that entity in the public interest, or what stops the operators from being exactly the platform owners the paper set out to remove. The upgrade path that can cut an author off "technically" is a lever, and levers get pulled. The paper's honesty about the lever is good; its silence about who holds it is not.

## Sharpest questions

- If the audience, the revenue, and the discoverability all live with the *name*, what exactly has the departing writer taken with them that makes the publication "have to persuade" them? Quantify the cost of leaving from the writer's side and the answer stops being symmetrical.
- Who, concretely and by name or role, runs the governors — and what incentive do they have to resist the censorship lever when the state or the mob comes asking, given that the draft itself concedes the lever exists?
- If source material is encrypted and only decrypted inside the generator under gates, what happens under a legal compulsion against the author for their keys? Walk the subpoena scenario end to end, or say plainly that this is outside scope — but say it in the section where pseudonymity is discussed, not buried.
- Is the DNS risk a caveat or a design limit? If the ENS name is the single point at which both a state and a hostile publication can apply pressure, why is the paper's treatment of it three sentences long?
- "Neither party is paid from the other's hand" — but the *publication* holds the revenue address and decides distribution timing and policy. In what sense is that not still a paymaster for the writer whose readership lives behind the domain?

## Suggested changes

- **Add a §3 or §4 paragraph that confronts the audience/domain asymmetry directly.** Admit that the writer leaves with their bytes and not their readers, and then make the honest argument for why the protocol still shifts leverage — for example, that a publication whose *reputation* is staked on not being a host that traps contributors is itself disciplined by the possibility that every contributor could leave at once, and that the threat of *collective* exit is the real mechanism. That is a stronger claim than "both sides lose" and it is the one the design actually supports.
- **Rewrite the §6 censorship paragraph without the child-pornography example.** Say the lever exists, say it is public, say where it sits. Then make the honest structural point the draft is circling: that any upgrade path is a governance problem, and that the protocol's protection is that the decision cannot be silent. Remove the volunteered example; let the reader supply the horror stories and notice that the draft is not choosing for them.
- **Move the DNS discussion from a caveat to a named risk subsection in §3** with its own paragraph on mitigations (multi-address records, mirrors, the fact that a withdrawn name does not revoke the content-addressed copies) — and state clearly what the protocol will not do for the name.
- **Cut the "proof of humanity / administrative identity" teaser from §7 unless a mechanism paragraph follows it.** One sentence raising the most sensitive feature in the entire paper, then moving on, is worse than omitting it.
- **Replace one of the repeated "pseudonymity is an option, not a promise" formulations in §2 with the actual threat model** the phrase gestures at: who can learn what, under what process, and what the protocol cannot prevent. Repetition currently substitutes for analysis.

## What could be shortened or left out

The single paragraph I would cut is the final paragraph of **Section 1 (Introduction)**: *"There is a future for online publishing in which association and dissociation between groups of people are easier and involve less friction: temporary collectives formed around topics and interests, bringing together a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer."*

It is mood, not argument. The introduction has already made its case — the host/guest asymmetry, the accumulated disposition, the separation of judgment from position — and it closes with a concrete, argued vision of what the protocol does. This last paragraph abandons the specific for the aspirational ("temporary collectives," "plurality of opinions," "insights only diversity can offer") without adding a single mechanism, cost, or condition. It is the kind of sentence that belongs in a pitch deck, and it is the only one in the draft. Trimming it leaves the introduction ending on the strongest line it has — "An editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot" — which is where the reader's attention should stop.

Secondary cut: the **abstract** restates that same closing sentiment twice ("a relationship that is real and temporary at once" and "an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot"). The abstract should not double the intro's flourish; keep one, drop the other, so the paper does not begin and end on the same rhetorical beat. If forced to choose a single trim, cut the abstract's second flourish and let the intro own it.
