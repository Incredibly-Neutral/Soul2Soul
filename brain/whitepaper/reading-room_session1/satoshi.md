# Satoshi — reading-room note (round 2)

## Verdict

Stronger than round 1, and the structure is now right: the abstract promises a protocol, and §§2–7 deliver sections that each carry a trust-bearing claim instead of a mechanism. The three-gate spine (§5) and the read-path verification (§7) are the two things that should have been there all along, and the paper is materially better for them. But the draft now makes three guarantees it does not fully support, and each is a spot where a reader with a key and a week of time would poke a hole: (1) "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone," which the governor upgrade path in §6 quietly softens; (2) "the gate is enforced by the machinery itself... not by any party's decision," which is only true until the governors change the code; and (3) the claim that leaving now "costs both sides," which the protocol does not actually deliver. These are not fatal — they are honestly half-named in the text — but the paper currently names them and then hedges in a way that a careful reader will notice. Verdict: accept the structure, tighten the guarantees, and cut the repetition.

## What resonates

The core move is correct and it is the whole paper: separate editorial judgment from the position it currently rides on. "The line is held by position, not argument, and no one had to choose that: it follows from the price of leaving" is the best sentence in the document, because it is the actual complaint. The platform's problem was never that it lied — its terms were as visible as any on-chain record — it was that leaving was expensive. The protocol's real contribution is to lower the author's cost of exit, and the draft knows this.

"Ending an agreement is a fact rather than a request" (Abstract, §4) is exactly the right distinction, and it is the one thing a blockchain genuinely adds over a contract hosted by a party. Neither party operates the record; neither can rewrite it; there is no host to pull. That is the honest reason for the chain, and it is stated plainly.

The gate section is correctly framed as machinery, not policy: "enforced by the machinery itself — by what the code will do and what the key will sign." That is the right way to think about it, and the three gates (rerender / deal / can-read) are the right three. Making the money gate optional and admitting it is "not yet built" is the correct discipline — money is where dependency actually concentrates, and pretending otherwise would have been the false promise.

"Pseudonymity is an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes" is the right note to strike about anonymity, and the separation of the account from the legal name — "what the protocol does not hold is the name behind it" — is honest.

## Critical readings

**1. The "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone" claim does not survive §6.** The abstract and §1 make the strong statement: no single party — author, publication, operator — can produce a valid rendering alone. But §6 tells us the governors can adopt code that cuts a publication or an author off, "the upgrade path makes that technically possible." That is not a cosmetic caveat. If the governors can change what the generator may do, then the set of people who can produce a valid rendering *together* includes the governors, and the guarantee is really "no single party *other than the governors*." The draft names this honestly — "That is a limit of the design, named rather than hidden" — and that honesty is to its credit. But a guarantee that begins "no single party can" and ends "except the governors" is a weaker sentence than the opening claims. The reader of the abstract has been promised more than §6 delivers.

**2. "Enforced by the machinery itself" is only conditionally true.** §5 says the gate is enforced "not by any party's decision when the moment comes." That is true at the moment. It is not true across time, because §6's upgrade path lets the governors change what the machinery will do. The platform the intro criticizes also enforced its terms "at the moment" — the harm was the accumulation of changes over time, "each change too small to leave over, the sum of them an arrangement nobody agreed to." The governor upgrade path is the same mechanism, merely on-chain and visible. Visibility is not the differentiator the draft claims. The real complaint against the platform was not that its changes were hidden — they were in the TOS — it was that leaving was costly. So the draft needs to say plainly what the difference is: not "silent vs visible" but "leaving is cheap," and then it needs to show the upgrade path does not reintroduce a high exit cost. As written, it asserts the distinction ("a change is visible, never silent") without arguing why visibility fixes a problem that was never about visibility.

**3. "Departure costs both sides" is not delivered.** The intro's climax is that leaving now costs the publication too, because it has to persuade rather than replace. But look at what the protocol actually gives: a writer leaves and takes their work; the publication keeps its name, its domain, its subscriber list, its reputation. "It costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced" — the very sentence the intro uses to condemn the platform — is unchanged by this protocol. The publication's bargaining position is barely moved; only the author's is improved. That is a real improvement and worth having, but the draft overstates it as symmetric when it is asymmetric. Either soften the claim or show what the publication actually loses that it cannot replace.

**4. The protocol key is the unexamined trust root.** Everything turns on the protocol key: the generator can only decrypt and sign with it, and the contract admits writes only from it. The draft tells us the private part "exists only inside those actions, and is reached under on-chain conditions." This is the strongest and least-supported claim in the paper. Who holds that key, how is it shared, what stops a party with enough of it from signing a tree the gates would reject? If the answer is multi-party computation among the independent nodes, the draft should say so, because that is the actual answer to "no single party can produce a valid rendering alone" — and it is a claim about the key's distribution, not about the gates. Right now the paper gestures at the answer ("executed by independent nodes") without stating the key's custody model, and the guarantee cannot be checked without it. This is the one place the draft is not paranoid enough, and for a paper whose whole thesis is trust minimization, that is the wrong failure mode.

**5. Money is deferred, and that is where dependency lives.** The optional gate is honestly marked "not yet built," and I respect that. But the paper's own thesis is that publishing runs on dependency, and the deepest dependency is the paymaster: "neither party is paid from the other's hand." That claim appears in §1 as if the protocol delivers it. It does not yet — it reserves the space for it. A reader should not be told the paymaster is removed in §1 and only discover in §5 that the gate that removes it is optional and unbuilt. The abstract gets this right ("optional gate on reading, described here and not yet built"); §1 states the division as accomplished. Align §1 with the abstract's honesty.

**6. Verification's "nothing but the page" claim rides on a compromised path.** §7 says the reader verifies "using nothing but the page," and that is true of the signature. But the reader first has to *reach* the page through the domain, and §3 concedes the path is "prone to the risks of DNS: the name can be seized, poisoned, or made hard to resolve." So the honest statement is: the page proves its own integrity once you have it, but the route to it is not trustless. The draft knows this and states it, but the two claims sit in different sections and the paper never shows the reader what the combined guarantee is. A reader following the argument will feel the tension and deserve a sentence that reconciles "using nothing but the page" with "the name can be seized."

## Sharpest questions

- If the governors can adopt code that cuts off a publication or author, what is the difference between trusting the governors and trusting a platform? You have removed the platform and replaced it with a committee that holds the same upgrade lever. Visibility alone is not the answer — the platform's changes were visible too. Where is the exit-cost argument for the governors?
- Who physically holds the protocol key, and what is the minimum number of parties that must collude to sign a rendering the gates reject? "Independent nodes" is a distribution claim, not a custody claim. State the threshold.
- "Departure costs both sides" — what precisely does the publication lose when a writer leaves, that it cannot replace? If the answer is "nothing," the sentence should be cut.
- The platform's harm was described as "each change too small to leave over." The upgrade path makes changes *larger and visible* rather than small and incremental — does that actually change the calculus, or just the optics?
- On the serviced path, who pays for storage, rendering, and record writes, and what stops that payer from being the new paymaster? The pending who-pays note is machinery economics, but the dependency thesis means it is also trust economics.
- "Using nothing but the page" — how does a reader who cannot reach the page because the name is seized verify anything? What is the offline bootstrap, or is the DNS risk simply accepted?
- The abstract says ending an agreement is "a fact rather than a request." For the publication, is ending the author's agreement also a fact? §3 says the publication "declines to keep carrying" a work — does that require the author's agreement to end, or is it unilateral like the author's revocation? The symmetry the paper claims needs to be explicit.

## Suggested changes

1. **Fix the "no single party" guarantee to match §6.** Either weaken the abstract/§1 opening to the honest version — "no party other than the governors, who can only act publicly" — or commit to a design where the governors genuinely cannot (a build with no upgrade path). The paper already offers the no-upgrade version as an aspiration ("this protocol would be glad to be its inspiration"); the choice between the two should be made and the guarantee stated to match.
2. **State the protocol key's custody model in §6.** Replace "exists only inside those actions" with the actual threshold — how many nodes, what MPC scheme, what collusion floor. This is the load-bearing claim of the whole system and it currently lives in a half-sentence.
3. **Argue why visibility fixes what the platform did wrong, or drop the visibility claim.** The intro's real complaint is exit cost. Tie the upgrade path to exit cost: a governor action that hurts an author is visible *and* the author can leave cheaply. That is the actual defense; state it.
4. **Correct the symmetry of "departure costs both sides."** Acknowledge the asymmetry — the author's exit cost drops sharply, the publication's barely moves — and either own that as the honest claim or show what the publication loses.
5. **Align §1's money claim with the abstract.** §1 currently says the paymaster is removed; the abstract correctly says the gate that would do it is optional and unbuilt. Make §1 say "the protocol leaves room to remove the paymaster" rather than implying it is done.
6. **Add one reconciling sentence at the end of §7** acknowledging the DNS path: the page proves itself once reached, and the route to it is not trustless, which is why content-addressability and the record matter.

## What could be shortened or left out

The single strongest cut: **the repeated pseudonymity sentence.** The exact line — "pseudonymity is an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes" — appears **four times**: in the Abstract, in §1, in §2, and again in §7 ("pseudonymity remains an option"). §2 is the natural home for it, since it is where the account and identity are defined; the other three occurrences can be cut or reduced to a single clause. This is the cleanest trim in the draft: it is pure repetition, adds nothing new at any site, and the argument loses nothing — the four iterations are what make the draft read as hedging rather than conviction. Trim it to one.

Secondary, if a paragraph is wanted: **the final paragraph of §1** ("There is a future for online publishing in which association and dissociation... a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer") is aspirational and could be dropped without touching the argument — it is the one place the paper slides from protocol to vision-statement. It is not wrong, but it is not load-bearing, and the draft is otherwise disciplined about avoiding exactly this kind of flourish. Keep it only if the author wants the paper to end the introduction on a forward note; the argument does not need it.

Both cuts remove weight without removing reasoning. The pseudonymity repetition is the one I would insist on; the §1 closing is a judgment call.
