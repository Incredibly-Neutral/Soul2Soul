### Abstract

Publishing together runs on dependency. The writer's byline, income, and audience sit in whatever system the publication or the platform controls, so the side that can afford to walk away sets the terms. This paper proposes a publishing protocol in which neither party holds that position: authors keep cryptographic ownership of their work and can revoke it at any time; a publication is the set of agreements currently in force, recorded on a chain neither party operates; and the generator that renders and signs each page is open code bound to a key, executed by independent nodes, so no single party can produce a valid rendering alone. The protocol does not impose a payment model — an optional gate on reading, described here and not yet built, leaves room for one — and pseudonymity is an option it permits, not a promise it makes. The result is a relationship that is real and temporary at once: writers and publications associate on terms either can end, and an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot.

### 1. Introduction

Some journalists work a single story for years — shared sources, shared legal exposure, a joint byline that carries weight precisely because all of them stand behind it. Then a second story arrives, and they land on opposite sides of it. Not mildly but completely, past the point where the next piece could be co-signed. One of them is a guest on the other's platform, and has been the whole time.

Authors build a publication on a platform: an archive worth citing, a subscriber list, a reputation the platform did not create. The company behind it adjusts its terms a little at a time — a clause, a payout schedule, a content policy — each change too small to leave over, the sum of them an arrangement nobody agreed to.

News breaks on a subject perhaps forty people in the country understand, and they want to be in a room together by Friday, arguing and correcting each other in public. Before they can start, someone has to own the room.

What mechanism helps them dissociate? What is the cost of leaving? Who would be in charge? All three are the same question, and online publishing today answers it one way: someone holds the keys. A group of writers can accept a platform's terms, which can change or end without them, or run their own server, which places the site, the subscriber list, the archive, and the revenue in the hands of whoever holds the credentials. A falling-out costs the writer their income, their record, and the home of their byline; it costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced. The party with less to lose sets the terms, and everyone inside adjusts without being asked to. What accumulates is not a policy but a disposition — a publication that speaks with one voice and can point to no instance of having enforced it. The line is held by position, not argument, and no one had to choose that: it follows from the price of leaving.

The protocol's move is to separate editorial judgment from the position it currently rides on. An author keeps cryptographic ownership of the work and can revoke it at any time. A publication is the set of agreements currently in force — recorded on a chain neither party operates, so ending one is a fact rather than a request. The pages are rendered and signed by open code bound to a key and executed by independent nodes: no single party — author, publication, operator — can produce a valid rendering alone. The generator is governed, and the governing is public — governors choose which actions it accepts, and a change is visible, never silent. Coordination stays in human hands; the leverage passes to machinery with no interests. Editorial judgment survives. What goes is the leverage, which was never the craft.

What the protocol offers is deliberately narrow. It addresses the parties to a publication — the writers, the name, the readers — and not the state: courts can compel the parties to any arrangement, this one included, and the independence on offer is from each other, not from the law. It does not impose a payment model; it leaves room for one — a publication may set an optional gate on reading, described in this paper and not yet built — and dividing a payment does not make money harmless, but it removes the paymaster: neither party is paid from the other's hand. A byline may be a legal name or a pseudonym; pseudonymity is an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes.

To a reader, a publication is still a name that resolves and a page with a byline. The name authenticates what it serves, and each page carries its own proof of who wrote it, who published it, and that it has not been altered. What changes is the relationship underneath: departure costs both sides, because a publication that wants to keep its contributors has to persuade them, and a writer who stays is choosing the publication rather than avoiding the cost of leaving. An editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot. 

There is a future for online publishing in which association and dissociation between groups of people are easier and involve less friction: temporary collectives formed around topics and interests, bringing together a plurality of opinions, and the insights only diversity can offer.
