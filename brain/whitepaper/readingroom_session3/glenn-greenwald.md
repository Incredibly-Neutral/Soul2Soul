# Glenn Greenwald — reading-room note (round 3)

## Verdict

The draft has improved in exactly the direction my last note pushed, and it has done so without losing the honesty that made round 2 worth reading. The aspirational closing paragraph is gone from §1; the proof-of-humanity teaser is gone from §7; the material on censorship and on who holds the governance lever is still there, still unaddressed. The paper has grown a section (Money, §6) and a subsection (Paying for the machinery) that answer questions the previous draft had only gestured at. It is still, in my view, a genuine whitepaper rather than a pitch — the willingness to name its own limits remains its best quality, and the suppression/forgery distinction in §7 is the most honest paragraph in the entire document.

But the central sociological claim — the one everything else exists to serve — is still asserted rather than earned. The draft has added machinery, and machinery is not the missing argument. If I could make the author do one thing before this is called a whitepaper, it would be to confront the audience asymmetry head-on, in the section where the "both sides lose" claim is made. Everything else I have to say below is secondary to that.

## What resonates

- **The suppression/forgery distinction in §7.** This is new since my last note, and it is the strongest single addition. "Suppression is a public act a community may choose within the law; forgery would require the whole protocol to collude in a misrepresentation that is public at the same time, which defeats itself." That is the correct way to state what the protocol can and cannot promise, and "This is not a guarantee to lean on" is the correct amount of confidence. Whoever wrote this paragraph understood the difference between a technical claim and a moral one.
- **"Ending an agreement is a single act with immediate effect... the author does not ask, and the publication does not need to agree" (§4).** The permissionless exit, restated cleanly. It was the core of the protocol in round 2 and it still is. The sentence "What the ledger settles is what was agreed and when, not whether either party honored it" is an important and correct narrowing of what the machinery claims to do.
- **"Neither party is paid from the other's hand" is now backed by a real mechanism (§6).** The three rails — destination, division, disclosure — plus the explicit separation of rewards from "paying for the machinery" is a genuine improvement over the earlier hand-wave. The line "a for-profit publication can make this easy by folding the cost into a subscription... anyone who does not want that model... can bring their own" is honest about cost in a way most such papers are not.
- **The removal of the aspirational paragraph from §1.** It is gone, and the introduction now ends on "An editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot," which is where the reader's attention should stop. This was my specific cut request and it was taken. Good.
- **"What goes is the leverage, which was never the craft" (§1) still holds.** The separation of editorial judgment from the position it rides on is the moral core of the paper, and it survives contact with the added machinery.

## Strongest criticisms

1. **The reciprocity claim is still asserted, not proven — and the new machinery makes the absence more visible, not less.** §1 says "departure costs both sides," and §3 says "a publication that wants to keep its contributors must persuade them." Neither section has been changed to engage what I flagged in round 2 and what the draft's own text concedes: the revenue goes to the publication's address (§3), the readers resolve the domain, and the name is what the audience is bound to. The draft now explains in elaborate detail *how* a writer leaves — the Merkle tree, the revocations, the gates — but it has still not answered the question *why the publication must persuade anyone*. The scarce asset in this arrangement is audience, and the audience lives with the domain. The machinery makes the leaving frictionless; it does not make the leaving costless, and it does not move the audience. This is the paper's softest spot, unchanged. (The honest version of the argument — that a publication's *reputation* for not trapping contributors is itself a discipline, because every contributor could leave at once, and that the real lever is collective exit — is still the one the design actually supports, and it is still not in the paper.)

2. **The child-pornography paragraph (§7) is still there, still voluntary, still the trap.** It is nearly verbatim what I objected to in round 2: "child pornography is the obvious one; where the line sits is the judgment of the appointed group." My objection was not prudishness. It is that a press-freedom document should not hand the reader the most extreme censorable example and then say the censors get to decide. The paragraph's own framing — "that is a limit of the design, named rather than hidden" — is the frame the whole document needs, but the volunteered example undercuts it. You do not need to name the worst case to name the limit. Cut the example; keep the mechanism. Unchanged since my last note, and I still consider it the second-most important thing to fix.

3. **The "pseudonymity is an option, not a promise" formulation is still doing the work that a threat model should do.** §2 now has more material around it — the account is public, the name is not held, the author chooses whom to tell — but the document still does not walk the exposure scenario end to end: what precisely happens under a subpoena or a compelled-key demand against the author for their keys, given that source material is encrypted and only decrypted inside the generator under gates. The draft is honest that it is not a promise; it still does not say what it is. I do not require a legal analysis; I require the section to say, in the section where pseudonymity is discussed, either "this is the exposure model" or "this is outside the protocol's scope, and here is the boundary." Repetition of the disclaimer still substitutes for that.

4. **The DNS risk is still a caveat in a section, not the design limit it is.** §3 now gives the name's failure modes their own paragraph — "seized, poisoned, or made hard to resolve" — which is an improvement over the three sentences of round 2. It is still short, it still offers no mitigations (multi-address records, mirrors, the fact that content-addressed copies survive a withdrawn name), and it still does not say what the protocol will not do for the name. For a document whose entire thesis is the price of leaving, the single point at which both a hostile state and a hostile publication can apply pressure deserves more than one paragraph. The paper is honest about the risk; it is still dismissive of it.

5. **Governance is still "the choices of those who run it," and "who runs it" is still unanswered.** §7 says the paper "does not set" the number of governors or their threshold — only that the choices are explicit and public. I said in round 2 that "explicit and public" is a transparency feature, not a check on power, and the draft has not added anything since. Some entity with real people decides continuation, resilience, and censorship-resistance; the draft concedes the upgrade path can cut a publication or author off "technically." The honesty about the lever is good. The silence about who is plausibly motivated to hold it in the public interest, and what stops the operators from being the platform owners the paper set out to remove, is not. This is the third-most important thing to fix, and it is untouched.

## Sharpest questions

- The new machinery answers "how does a writer leave?" in detail. It still does not answer "what does the writer take with them?" If the audience, the revenue, and the discoverability all live with the domain, what precisely has the departing writer taken that forces the publication to persuade them — as opposed to the publication finding the next writer who wants the audience? Quantify the writer's cost of leaving and the symmetry claim either stands or falls.
- Who, concretely and by role, runs the governors, and what incentive do they have to resist the censorship lever when the state or the mob comes asking — given that §7 itself concedes the lever exists and can be pulled?
- Under the current §4/§5 design — source encrypted, decrypted only inside the generator under gates — walk the compelled-key scenario against the author end to end. If the answer is "that is outside the protocol's scope," fine; but it needs to be said in §2, not left for the reader to infer.
- If the ENS name is the single point where a state and a hostile publication can both apply pressure, why is its treatment one paragraph in a nine-section document?
- §6 now separates rewards from machinery costs and says a non-profit or hobbyist can "run the machinery themselves." Run that scenario: what does a lone non-profit actually operate, at what real cost, and does the answer still hold that "no third party is required"?

## Suggested changes

- **Write the audience paragraph.** In §1 or §3, admit the writer leaves with their bytes and not their readers, and then make the honest argument: the mechanism of discipline is not that each departure is costly, but that the *threat of collective departure* is — a publication whose reputation depends on not being a host that traps contributors is itself constrained, and the protocol converts an individual's trapped position into a standing, verifiable option that everyone can see on the record. That is the claim the design actually supports, and it is stronger than "both sides lose."
- **Cut "child pornography is the obvious one" from §7.** Keep the mechanism sentence, keep "that is a limit of the design, named rather than hidden," delete the volunteered example. Let the reader supply the worst case; the draft should not be choosing for them or inviting the censors to justify themselves.
- **Promote the DNS paragraph in §3 to a named subsection** with one paragraph on mitigations and one sentence on what the protocol will not do for the name. The name is the choke point; the paper should stop writing about it like a footnote.
- **Replace one repetition of "option, not promise" in §2 with the exposure model** — who can learn what, under what process, and where the protocol's protection ends. Repetition still substitutes for analysis.
- **State in §7 who plausibly runs the governors and what governs them.** Even a paragraph that says "the paper does not set this, and that is a deliberate gap; here is what we think a credible operator looks like and why" would be more than the current silence.

## What could be shortened or left out

- The abstract is now doing double duty: it restates the intro's closing flourish ("an editorial line held by people free to leave means something a captive one cannot") *and* adds "real and temporary at once." That was my round-2 complaint about the abstract doubling the intro's rhetoric, and it has gotten slightly worse with the added "real and temporary" phrasing. Keep the promise in the intro; let the abstract be the roadmap.
- §6's "Paying for the machinery" is genuinely new and honest, but it can be tightened by half: the second paragraph ("Anyone who does not want that model...") repeats the "no fiat on-ramp or off-ramp is required, and none is forbidden" construction that appears in the Abstract, §2, and §6 already. One statement of the fiat-agnosticism is enough; three is defensive.
- The gate numbering in §5 and §7 is slightly confusing — gates are introduced in §5 as "the first gate / the second gate / the third gate," and the third gate reappears in §6 and §7. If the gates are going to be the protocol's spine, they deserve to be named (render gate, deal gate, disclosure gate) rather than counted, because the reader keeps losing count.

## Where I have no documented view

To be explicit about the limits of this note rather than pretend otherwise: I have no documented position on the cryptographic mechanics — the TEE/runtime-sealing claims, the Merkle-tree rebuild and its collision behavior, the ENS content-hash resolution details, the smart-contract write-path restrictions. I have opinions about what the protocol *claims* socially and what it *concedes* structurally, and those are above. On whether the machinery as specified actually delivers the rendering-and-verification properties the paper promises, I am not the right reader, and I will not pretend the technical sections are sound or unsound. The same applies to the fee/gas economics of §6: the honesty about cost is a real improvement, but the actual numbers — what "pay your own way" costs a lone author in practice — are outside anything I have studied, and I flag that as an open question rather than a finding.
