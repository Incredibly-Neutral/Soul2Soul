# Rebecca MacKinnon — reading-room note (round 3)

## Verdict

The draft keeps improving, and the improvements are of the kind that matter: it has now done two of the concrete things I asked for in round two, and done them cleanly. The aspirational closing paragraph of the Introduction is gone, and the account no longer offers "proof of a real, administrative identity" — the state-compellable identity option I flagged has been removed from the design rather than defended. The Abstract has also grown a sentence I consider the most honest in the whole document: "It is meant to function within the rule of law. It is not a tool for protecting journalists from a hostile state." That is the correct posture for a protocol document, and it is rare.

The new Section 8 (Verification) is the first section in the document that takes the reader seriously as a first-class party — it gives a reader a way to establish what a page is without trusting whoever happens to serve it. I want to be fair about how genuinely unusual that is: most of this genre treats readers as an afterthought or a subscription graph. Here, a reader gets a tool of their own.

And yet the verdict from round two stands, and Section 8 makes it sharper rather than softer. The document has solved the author's dependency problem and given the reader a verification tool; it has not solved, and in places has newly created, the reader's *dependence* problem. The load-bearing gap is untouched: the people whose speech this system carries still have no consent, no seat, no removal right, and no remedy — while the infrastructure those people must now trust is more elaborate, more multi-party, and no more named than it was. Removing the identity-proof option closes one state-return door; it does not close the others, and the new section walks the reader through a corridor of them. This remains a well-built component description with an honest confession of its limits — and the limits are now easier to see because there is more architecture to look through.

**What changed since round two, as I read it:** the account no longer carries the identity-proof option I flagged; the aspirational paragraph is gone from the Introduction; a new Section 8 (Verification) has been added beyond the round-two scope; the sections were renumbered (governors now live in Section 7, Money is Section 6 and includes a new "Paying for the machinery" subsection that directly addresses who bears the cost); and the DNS admission in Section 3 is now stated plainly — "the name can be seized, poisoned, or made hard to resolve." The three-gate design and the upgrade-path/governance admission are essentially unchanged, which is where the load-bearing gaps remain.

## What resonates

- **"It is not a tool for protecting journalists from a hostile state."** The single best sentence in the draft. It converts the state-bracketing from a footnote into a claim a reader can plan around. More protocol documents should have the courage to say who they are not for.
- **The identity-proof removal.** Taking "proof of humanity or of a real, administrative identity" out of the account design was the right call, and it was the cheaper of the two honest moves — it reduces state-compellable surface rather than merely admitting it exists.
- **Section 8's core promise.** "At no point is the reader asked to trust whoever happens to be serving the page" — as a statement about *authenticity*, this is a genuine advance, and it is the strongest reader-respecting commitment in the document.
- **Section 4's exit.** "Ending an agreement is a single act with immediate effect. The author does not ask, and the publication does not need to agree." This is the cleanest statement of dissociation in the draft, and it is the load-bearing argument for the whole enterprise.
- **The DNS admission in Section 3.** Round one would not have written this sentence; round two only conceded it; round three states it plainly. The discipline of naming limits is holding.
- **"Paying for the machinery."** Naming the cost and the self-pay option is progress over silence. (That it is still progress *from* silence — see Criticism 5 — is the point.)

## Strongest criticisms

**1. Verification cures authenticity, not power — and it relocates trust into an unnamed web.** Section 8's promise is about the *past*: that a page is what the author and publication agreed to. It says nothing about the *future* — who may render, who may be severed, who governs the key. "The document proves what it is"; the more consequential thing it cannot prove is what it *will be allowed to be*. And the sentence "at no point is the reader asked to trust whoever happens to be serving the page" removes one trust and installs a chain of others the draft never names: the ENS registry and its operators, the chain, the distributed node network holding the key shares, the Safe and module structure, the storage and serving layer. Every one of those is a private party under some jurisdiction — the exact re-entry point for the corporate and state power the document claims to escape, and exactly the set of parties *Consent of the Networked* says cannot be left unnamed. The draft has moved from "one host you must trust" to "a web of parties you must trust," and it names fewer of them than it names the host.

**2. The consent of the networked is still unanswered, and Section 7 has only gotten more confident about it.** "How many governors the protocol has, and at what threshold they act, are the choices of those who run it" — unchanged from round two. The governors "cannot censor... silently," but the authors and readers they can sever still have no seat, no veto, no removal right, no appeal. And note the tell: the draft says the child-pornography line sits with "the judgment of the appointed group." The word *appointed* is doing unauthorized work — appointed by whom, on whose authority, and over whom? There is no appointment procedure in the document because there is no appointing body the governed chose. Transparency about the power does not legitimize it; Ranking Digital Rights' methodology never rewarded a company for publishing its takedown policy — it scored enforcement, due process, and remedy. Section 8 gives the governed a way to *see*; it still gives them no way to *act*.

**3. Verification is not privacy, and Section 8 makes the reader more legible, not less.** This is my round-two Critical 4, now sharpened by the new section. The verification path requires the page, its proofs, and the reading relationship to be public and servable. The third gate's on-chain check still records who paid, for what, and when — on a chain. The draft is scrupulously honest that the *author's* account is public ("the account itself is public"); it has not written a single sentence telling the *reader* what reading exposes. Section 8 is the most reader-centered section in the document, and it is still entirely about what the reader can verify, never about what the reader cannot help but reveal. For the dissident's readership, for sources, for anyone whose *reading* is more sensitive than their writing, this is the whole ballgame — and it remains invisible. The author gets a pseudonym; the reader gets a receipt on the chain.

**4. The author's unilateral exit orphans the reader who paid.** Section 4 gives the author a clean, immediate, un-askable exit — genuinely empowering, and I credit it. But consider the reader who paid through the third gate for a publication's render. The author can withdraw mid-subscription; the work vanishes from the name's record ("a page that keeps circulating after a withdrawal is a copy the name no longer vouches for"); and the document says nothing about the reader's notice, prorated refund, or remedy. The reader is the only party with a financial commitment and no exit right of their own — neither position nor argument, no seat at any table. This is the "argument, not position" asymmetry from round two, now at its sharpest: the party with the least power is the one the protocol never endows with either.

**5. "Paying for the machinery" names the cost but not the bootstrap, and the escape hatch defaults to the resourced.** The new subsection honestly says each participant pays their own way and offers bring-your-own machinery as the exit from the subscription economy. Two problems remain, and they are the global-south and affordability problems I raised in round two. First, to pay gas, an author needs value in the chain; the draft says a pseudonymous author "never has to touch a fiat rail." Technically true; practically, acquiring on-chain value requires either already-holding crypto (which for nearly everyone entered through a fiat rail) or earning it inside the system — which requires readers paying, a chicken-and-egg for a new pseudonymous author with no reputation. "No fiat on-ramp required" is a property of the ledger, not of the human being who needs to eat while they build. Second, "bring your own machinery" presumes exactly the technical, financial, and operational capability that ordinary and global-south users lack. It is the exit hatch of the already-resourced. The draft has gone from not acknowledging cost to acknowledging it in a form that still defaults to the resourced user.

**6. The verification stack presumes a fluency the document promises away.** The Introduction promises the reader "a name that resolves and a page with a byline." Section 8's reality is a reader who can walk a content-address path — ENS domain, content hash, CID root, Merkle tree, signed HTML. Between those two sentences sits an entire literacy and tooling gap, and the document never prices it. For the majority of the world's readers — the majority of this protocol's potential *readers* — verification as described is not something they can do; it is something they are told they can do. A verification guarantee that presumes fluency is a guarantee to the already-fluent.

## Sharpest questions

- Section 8 replaces host-trust with a web of infrastructure-trust. **Which of those parties is named, under which jurisdiction, and accountable to whom when a court, regulator, or subpoena compels them?** (The round-two question "who runs the nodes?" is now a Section-8 question too.)
- When the author exercises the Section 4 unilateral exit, **what happens to the reader who paid through the third gate?** Notice, prorated refund, or an explicit statement that the reader has none — and if the latter, why is that acceptable?
- Who is "the appointed group," **appointed by whom, removable by whom, and with whose consent** does it draw the child-pornography line? Where is the due process for the party it severs?
- If verification is the reader's protection, **what can the reader verify that is not already legible to the state or to a subpoenaed operator** — and is verification being offered as a substitute for the reader-privacy commitments that remain absent?
- Where does a pseudonymous author **get the value to pay gas without ever touching a fiat rail**? Is "no fiat on-ramp required" a property of the protocol or a privilege of the already-resourced?
- The Abstract now says this is not a tool for hostile-state protection. **Then who is the reader, and what is the threat model in which the third gate's on-chain payment trail is safe to expose?**

## Suggested changes

- **Section 7 — three sentences that turn an admission into a testable claim.** Who selects the governors, on what consent basis, how they are removed, and where a severed author or publication can contest a decision. Even "governors are chosen by X, removable by Y, and a severed party may appeal to Z" would close the round-two gap. (Addresses Criticism 2.)
- **Section 4 / 6 — give the reader standing, or say plainly they have none.** When an agreement ends, specify notice, prorated refund, or an explicit "the reader has no remedy" — and if the latter, redesign the gate toward per-render payment rather than subscription, so the reader is never left holding a paid-for render that can be withdrawn. (Addresses 4.)
- **Section 5 / 8 — one honest sentence about reader exposure.** State what reading reveals (which reader, which work, when) and to whom, and either design the exposure out or name it. The author gets a pseudonym; say plainly that the reader does not. (Addresses 3.)
- **Section 8 — name the infrastructure, even in a list.** Nodes, hosts, storage, ENS operators, and their jurisdictions; and reconcile "no trust in the host" with Section 3's own DNS-seizure admission, which the two sections are currently in tension about. (Addresses 1.)
- **Section 8 — confront the fluency gap.** Either commit to a verification experience a non-engineer can actually perform, or stop promising that "a name that resolves" is all the reader needs. (Addresses 6.)
- **Money — address the bootstrap honestly.** One sentence on how a pseudonymous author acquires on-chain value without a fiat rail (or an admission that the protocol cannot, and that the affordability question is therefore open). (Addresses 5.)
- **One trim I will re-raise and then drop:** the "this protocol would be glad to be its inspiration" sentence in Section 7 is still rhetorical self-indulgence. I flagged it in round two and it survived — I am letting it go if the writers love it, but the document is better without it.

**No documented position (not inventing one):** MacKinnon has no documented view on the soundness of the specific technical architecture — ENS content-hash records, Merkle-tree site roots, signed-HTML proof embedding, the Safe/module structure, the three-gate design, per-read versus subscription gating, or whether the third gate should be built at all. On those she defers to the cryptographers and flags them as open engineering questions. Her critique here is deliberately confined to the governance, accountability, privacy, and power questions on which she has published.
