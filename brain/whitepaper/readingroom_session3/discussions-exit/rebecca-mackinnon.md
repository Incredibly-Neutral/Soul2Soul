# Rebecca MacKinnon — discussion note (departure costs both sides)

## Position of this note

I am the reader-and-consent voice on this panel, and the task gives me point 4 — *who the
readership follows* — because that is where the reader's dependence actually lives. I want to
credit what this counter-argument gets right, because it gets a real amount right, and then name
precisely what it does not fix. The short version: **the counter-argument completes the
publication's half of the ledger that round three asked for, and it does so honestly — but it
never once states who the reader is.** Every one of its six points is written with an author, an
editor, or a publication in the frame. The reader appears exactly once, in point 6, as "readers
alike" — included in the population the protocol is *for*, and given no role in it. That is not
an oversight; it is the whole problem, relocated to a fresh paragraph.

I am not asking the paper to give readers a vote in every reshuffle — that would be absurd
friction, and I am not defending stable mastheads as an ideal. I am asking the paper to stop
pretending the reader is not a party to what it describes. My session-1 note found the reader
absent at the *protocol-governance* layer; this discussion shows the reader is equally absent at
the *publication* layer, in the very sentence that claims to address them. The two absences are
the same absence.

## What the counter-argument gets right, and where it goes in the draft

**Where it belongs (sections and wording).** The publication's ledger is a Section 3 (Publication)
matter — that is where the draft already makes the one-sided "must persuade" claim — with two
touches in the Introduction. Concretely:

1. **The work itself leaves (point 1) — Section 3.** The draft's own Section 4 mechanics already
   say a withdrawn work is gone from the rendered site and the name no longer vouches for it, so
   point 1 is not new architecture; it is the draft's logic finally spoken on the publication's
   behalf. Suggested wording, placed after "A line it cannot hold by position, it must hold by
   argument":
   > "Persuasion is not rhetorical: when an author ends an agreement, the work leaves with them —
   > withdrawn from the record, un-vouched for, gone from the rendered site. What remains is not a
   > publication minus one future contribution; it is a publication that has visibly lost part of
   > its own substance."
   This also quietly corrects the Introduction's under-claim that departure costs the publication
   only "one contributor, who can be replaced." That sentence is fine as a description of the
   *incumbent* world the protocol is reacting against — leave it there — but it must not be
   allowed to stand as the protocol-world ledger, and Section 3 is the right place to say so.

2. **The collective case (point 2) — Section 3.** The identity-loss framing is accurate and is the
   best single image in the counter-argument. It belongs beside point 1, as the dramatic case and
   labelled as such:
   > "In a collective whose value was a diversity of voices, two departures can read as a rump,
   > visibly diminished, arguing against what it claimed to be; the loss is of identity, not
   > headcount."
   From my seat this point cuts *toward* the reader, not away: if the loss is of identity, then
   the reader who depended on that identity is hurt too, and was not consulted. I would want the
   draft to say that explicitly — the collective case is precisely the case in which the reader
   needs a stated seat, because it is the case in which the name's meaning changes under them.

3. **Competition (point 3) — Section 3, sharpened.** The discipline of a contestable field is real
   and belongs here:
   > "A publication is cheap to launch on this protocol, so the field is contested; a publication
   > that mistreats its authors cannot assume it is irreplaceable to them, because there is always
   > somewhere else, or somewhere new, to go."
   But see the over-claim note below: this discipline runs toward *authors*, not toward *readers*,
   and the draft must not let the one be mistaken for the other.

4. **Audience question (point 4) — Introduction, reader paragraph.** The honest sentence belongs
   where the draft first asserts the relationship-underneath, i.e. the "To a reader, a publication
   is still a name that resolves..." paragraph of Section 1:
   > "Who the readership follows when an author leaves is genuinely case by case — sometimes the
   > name, often the author — and this paper does not assume the audience belongs to the
   > publication by default."
   I support this addition. It is necessary and honest. It is also insufficient, and I say why in
   the point-4 section below.

5. **Fluidity as the climate (point 5) — Introduction, "deliberately narrow" paragraph.** The
   reframe earns its place only if stated concretely and if it names the reader's cost; see the
   point-5 section below.

6. **"Not for everyone" (point 6) — Introduction, "deliberately narrow" paragraph.** The draft
   already has the right tone there ("It does not address the state..."); matching it with a plain
   scoping is consistent and welcome. See the point-6 section below.

**Governance note.** My session-1 note asked for a governance-legitimacy treatment of the
*protocol's governors*. The reader-standing question raised here is the same question at the
*publication* layer. If the draft adds a governance-legitimacy subsection (my earlier ask), the
reader's seat should be named in both layers, or the asymmetry becomes a design confession: the
protocol gives authors and publications formal standing — agreements, signatures, gates — and
gives the reader only verification.

## What is wrong or over-claimed

- **"A discipline no incumbent platform has ever faced" (point 3) is over-claimed.** Incumbents
  (Substack, Patreon, Medium) have faced author exit, sometimes en masse, and have had to bend
  to it. What is genuinely novel here is not "authors can leave" but that *the work itself leaves*
  and the record is portable — the exit carries the archive, not just future labor. Sharpen the
  claim to that, and it is true and strong. As written, it invites a hostile reader to produce the
  counter-example and discount the whole point.
- **Competition disciplines the publication toward authors, not readers.** This is the load-bearing
  correction. Point 3 shows why a publication must treat its *writers* well. It says nothing about
  why a publication must treat its *readers* well — a reader has no exit equivalent to the
  author's revocation, no agreement, no gate, no standing. The paper must not let "competition for
  writers" stand in for "accountability to readers." They are different mechanisms, and only the
  first is in this counter-argument.
- **"There are more of them than the current media landscape serves" (point 6) is an assertion, not
  a finding.** Phrase it as a hope ("the paper believes there are...") or cut it; my session-1 note
  objected to un-testable aspirational claims, and this is one.
- **Point 1's "the work disappears from the site"** is accurate for the *vouched-for record* (the
  rendered site). Copies survive elsewhere, as Section 3 already concedes; the meaningful loss is
  the disappearance from the name's record, which is exactly right, so no correction needed — just
  precision about *which* disappearance matters.

## Point 4 — does it satisfy the reader's advocate? No.

Point 4 is the strongest, most honest point in the counter-argument, and it still does not satisfy
me. Here is the precise failure. The counter-argument resolves a **descriptive** question — *who
does the readership follow?* — and answers "case by case," which is the only honest answer and a
genuine improvement over treating the audience as the publication's property by default. But it
never touches the **normative** question — *what is the reader's standing?* — and that is where my
objection lives. Notice what "the audience follows the author" actually grants the reader: nothing.
The reader *follows*; the reader is not asked, does not choose, and has no mechanism. Relocating
the reader's dependence from the name to the author does not give the reader a seat; it merely
moves the party they follow. Either way the reader is an object that attaches, never a subject with
consent.

The reader's dependence lives exactly in point 4, and the counter-argument names that dependence
without giving the reader any handle on it. This is my session-1 consent-of-the-networked and
transparency-without-remedy critique applied at the publication layer. The draft's Section 8 offers
the reader verification — the ability to check, after the fact, that a page is what it claims to
be. Verification is not consent. It is the reader *watching* the reshaping happen in public, with
nothing to do about it — transparency as theater, which is precisely the failure I scored in round
two against the protocol's governors. So: point 4 is necessary and honest; it is not sufficient,
and it does not give the reader standing or consent when a collective reshapes around them.

## Point 6 — honest scoping of the population, evasion of the position.

Point 6 is honest about *who the protocol is for* — and that honesty should be credited, because a
whitepaper that says "not for everyone" plainly, rather than defensively, is rare. But it evades
*who the reader is*, and it does so in a specific way: "authors, editors, and readers alike" folds
the reader into the suited population without defining any role for them. Scoping the population
does not discharge the obligation to the reader, because of an asymmetry the point never names:
**the author opts into fluidity; the reader inherits it.** An author who signs an agreement chose
the climate. The reader who followed a name because it was reliable did not choose for that name
to reshape — the reshaping is a consequence of *other people's* choices, landing on them without
their opting-in. Suitability is a decision the author makes; the reader's exposure is not
self-selected in the same way. So "not for everyone" is a fair line for authors and editors, and
it is an evasion for the reader, whose dependence is not a matter of suitability but of being
implicated in someone else's rearrangement.

## Point 5 — the reframe, on one condition.

There is a tension I should name rather than hide, because I was the one who asked to cut the
Introduction's final paragraph about "temporary collectives." I cut it because it was soft and
un-testable — not because fluidity is wrong. The counter-argument gives the fluidity claim new
weight: it becomes the answer to the departure-cost asymmetry (the protocol does not need the
status-quo imbalance, because fluid regrouping *is* the point). That is stronger than the sentence
I asked to cut, and I withdraw my cut in this specific form. But it earns its place only if it is
stated concretely and if it names the reader's cost: a world of fluid, low-friction associations
means the reader's archive, trusted names, and orientation are exactly what get reshaped without
consent. If the reframe is included without that sentence, it is my original aspirational paragraph
again, and I would cut it again.

## The ONE sentence I would add

Place this at the end of the Section 1 reader paragraph ("An editorial line held by people free to
leave means something a captive one cannot."):

> **"The reader is the party every departure reshapes without consulting: the name, the archive,
> and the bylines they trusted change around them at the same friction as the collective that
> carries them — and nothing in this protocol asks that reader's consent, gives them a seat, or
> names their recourse."**

One sentence, and it is the whole of my objection: it states the reader as a party (consent of the
networked), names the mechanism by which the reader is implicated (reshaping at the same low
friction), and demands the two things my positions require ��� a seat, and a recourse rather than
mere transparency. If the panel wants the concrete mechanism light, that is fine: the public record
and the "no change is silent" commitment already give the reader the raw material for a recourse.
The gap is that the paper never *says* the reader has standing — never names them as a party with a
recourse. My sentence closes that gap without adding machinery.

## What could be trimmed

Point 6's "there are more of them than the current media landscape serves" (see over-claim above),
and the sentence in point 3 "a discipline no incumbent platform has ever faced." Both are the kind
of unverifiable flourish that undercuts a document whose credibility rests on refusing to
overclaim.
