# Discussion note — "departure costs both sides" counter-argument

Max Blumenthal (The Grayzone) — responding as the publication operator who owns the audience.

_Scope: the counter-argument to the panel's point that the paper asserts "departure costs both
sides" but only ever shows the author's half of the ledger. I ground my position in my own
session-1 note, where the readership gap is documented (§3 "Critical readings," sharpest
question 4, and the suggested change "Give the readership a home"); I have not read the
session-2 or session-3 notes. Where a claim in the counter-argument is not something I can
document a view on, I say so and mark it inference._

## Verdict

The counter-argument is mostly right, and for the right reason: it finally writes the
publication's half of the ledger on the publication's own terms. Points 1, 2, and 4 are the
substance; 3, 5, and 6 are framing. One part is over-claimed (point 3), one needs precision
before it goes in the paper (point 1's "the work disappears"), and point 4 — the one that is
mine — does the minimum I asked for and still stops short of the settlement I actually need.

## Point by point

**1. Losing the work itself: correct, and grounded in the draft's own mechanics.** The draft
already commits to this in §4: "a work is published because an agreement covers it, and ceases
to be published when that agreement ends," and §3 confirms a withdrawn work "may survive in
copies elsewhere, and the name does not vouch for it." So when an author walks, the work leaves
the publication's record. The one overstatement to fix before it reaches the paper: the work
does not "disappear from the site" the way "disappear from the world" would read — it leaves the
publication's record and loses the publication's voucher. Say it precisely and point 1 is not a
claim, it is the draft's own §4 in plain language. As an operator I feel this directly: §1
itself names "an archive worth citing" as an asset the platform did not create, and the archive
is exactly what drains when agreements end.

**2. The collective case: right instinct, illustrative scenario.** The identity-loss point is
real and belongs in the paper — a publication that visibly loses members is not the same
publication, and the loss is not proportional to headcount. But the specific "eight authors, two
walk out" is the counter-arguer's scenario, not one I can document a view on; mark it inference.
It is also the friendliest case for the "both sides" claim: a star-brand outlet where the
departure is total (the byline IS the brand) is the harder case, and it is the one my side
lives in. The paper should state the principle, not lean on the collective example.

**3. Setup cost falls, competition rises: over-claimed, and mislabeled.** This is not the
publication's cost; it is the author's leverage restated. It repeats what §3 already says
("there is always somewhere else to go") and it cuts against the symmetry the reply is trying
to build: if launching a publication is cheap, then the publication is cheap to replace too, and
the scarcity that remains is the audience — which brings the whole question back to point 4.
The empirical claim that this protocol yields a materially more contested field with real
disciplining force is something I have no documented view on; treat it as speculation. Drop it
from the ledger, or fold it into point 5's climate.

**4. The audience question: does it satisfy me? No, not fully — and I want to be precise about
why.** This is my gap, pressed from my side of the table. To be fair first: it does the minimum
I actually demanded. My session-1 note asked the paper to "state, somewhere in §3 or §4, who
retains the audience when an agreement ends — or explicitly concede that the protocol does not
address it." The counter-argument does the second of those: it concedes the paper has been
treating the audience as something the publication holds by default and says that treatment
should stop. That is real progress and I credit it. But then it stops at "genuinely case by
case," and as the operator whose asset the audience is, that is exactly where my hardest
question sits. The paper has built a model in which the author carries their work out the door
with full portability, and now concedes the audience may follow them — so the honest end-state
is that in many departures the publication loses BOTH the work AND the audience, and is left
holding only the name. In those cases "departure costs both sides" is not a balanced sentence;
it is a euphemism for "the publication loses everything it built with that author." Point 4
satisfies me as a concession and fails me as a settlement. I do not think a protocol can settle
who keeps a reader — loyalty is not assignable by an agreement — but the paper must say that in
full, naming the stakes, rather than sheltering in "case by case."

**5. The fluid-collectives reframe: acceptable mood, dangerous timing.** As a description of
what the protocol builds it is fine, and it matches the draft's own §1 aspiration. But placed
where the reply puts it — after four points about real losses — it invites the reader to
conclude the losses are trivial because the groupings were temporary. They are not trivial to
the person losing the audience. Sequence it after the costs are admitted, not as an eraser for
them. My own session-1 concern was continuity: my audience is built over years, and "fluid,
temporary" underplays what an owner is actually giving up. Keep the reframe as an offer, not a
dismissal.

**6. Not for everyone: correct, and the draft should say it plainly.** It matches the draft's
"deliberately narrow" framing and it is the honest way to stop being defensive. No quarrel.

## Where it goes in the draft, and with what wording

The claim lives in two places, and both need the ledger.

**§1 (Introduction), final paragraph.** The sentence "departure costs both sides, because a
publication that wants to keep its contributors has to persuade them…" asserts the claim with
only the author's half in support. Worse, three paragraphs up, the draft has already priced the
publication's loss as "it costs the publication one contributor, who can be replaced" — that is
the loaded line the counter-argument is answering. Fix the ledger in §1 by replacing the
one-sided support with both halves:

> "What changes is the relationship underneath: departure costs both sides. It costs the author
> the home of a byline and the reach the publication lent it; it costs the publication the
> work, gone from its record, and often the audience that came with it — and a publication that
> wants to keep its contributors must persuade them, just as it must keep earning its readers."

**§3 (Publication).** This is where the publication's half belongs as substance, and where my
note always said the readership question belonged. The paragraph "The publication does not own
the work — that is what makes it worth joining. Its contributors can leave at any time, taking
their work with them, so a publication that wants to keep them must persuade them" states the
author's leverage and nothing else. Append the publication's ledger right there:

> "Departure is not one-sided: a contributor who leaves takes their work out of the record and
> may take the audience with them, and the audience is not an asset this protocol assigns to
> either side. A publication that would keep its writers must also keep its readers, by
> argument and by the work itself."

**§4 (Agreements).** One line grounding the work-loss in the mechanics already there. Where the
draft says "a work is published because an agreement covers it, and ceases to be published when
that agreement ends," add: "and each such ending is a visible depletion of the publication's
record, not a convenience to it."

## The ONE sentence I would add

Grounded in my documented position — the readership gap I pressed in session 1 (the audience is
the asset with no home in the model; a contributor is not trivially replaceable when they leave
with the audience the publication helped them build):

> "The audience is the one asset the protocol cannot carry for either party: it belongs to
> neither by default, follows whoever earns it, and when a contributor leaves with it the
> publication loses everything that departure can take — which is why a line held by people
> free to leave, and readers free to follow them, means something a captive one cannot."

That sentence does three things the paper currently does not: it puts the audience into the
model as an asset, it concedes the audience is not the publication's by default (point 4's own
demand), and it makes "both sides" honest by refusing to pretend the losses are equal.

## What I cannot document (inference)

- The collective "eight authors, two walk out" scenario and its "rump state" psychology — the
  counter-arguer's illustration, not my documented situation.
- The claim that cheap setup produces a materially more contested field that disciplines
  incumbents (point 3) — no documented view; treat as speculation.
- Whether "in many cases the audience follows the author." In my own case the publication and
  the founder are so fused that "name vs author" is nearly a false dichotomy — but that is an
  operator's judgment about reader behavior, not a documented datum, and the paper should not
  rely on the "many cases" figure.
- Point 5's claim that the fluid climate is "more lively, more aspirational" — a mood, not an
  argument; flag it as such.

## Where the counter-argument changes my position

It moves me one step. It gets the paper to concede that the audience is not the publication's by
default — the thing I asked for. It does not answer who keeps the readers, because no honest
protocol can, and I no longer think the paper should pretend to. What it must do instead is the
sentence above: name the audience as an unassigned asset and let the asymmetry stand in the
open, rather than a "both sides" that quietly means "the publication loses everything."
