# Ursula K. Le Guin — reading-room note (round 3)

Persona lens: the novelist who argued in round 1 for the carrier-bag vision, the overlapping memberships, the portable byline — "a room everyone can leave is a room worth arguing in," "keep it a door, not a house." Question before me: **is the central agreements contract an improvement over the draft's per-publication design?** Where does it help, where does it fall short, what are the risks? As before, I have no documented position on the technical mechanics; my remarks on those are by analogy from what I have written on containers, power, and tools, and I say so at the end.

---

## Verdict

It is an improvement, and I would take it — but not whole. It moves in exactly the direction I asked for: the portable byline made real, the carrier bag made literal. The cross-referenced dual record is the best door in the design, and I would keep that with both hands. But it does not get out of the house. It answers the fragmentation it names by building a bigger center — a single ledger that everything must answer to — and it calls a veil a right. Take the door. Question the house. Name the veil. On the balance, it helps more than it hurts, but only if we refuse the center it quietly proposes.

## What the proposal gets right

**The portable byline, actually carried.** The disease the author diagnoses is real and I recognize it: the writer's history scattered across each publication's machinery, no place where "what this author has published, where, and for how long" can be seen as a whole. The fragmenting is a real harm — it is exactly the guest who was "still a guest," her record living in rooms she does not own. A single query that returns the author's whole record — every article, the publication it ran on, the window of the agreement — is the carrier bag made literal: you carry your work, not the publication. This is what I meant by a portable byline, and the draft's per-publication design could not give it. Good. Fix the fragmentation; it was a real disease and this is a real treatment.

**The cross-referenced dual record — the door.** This is the strongest move in the proposal, and the one I would defend. The author's own self-hosted archive and the neutral ledger corroborate each other: the same work appears in the author's record and in the publication's vouched record. Neither is the master. The two hold each other in tension, and the work is not answerable to either alone. That is the plurality preserved. It is a door — you pass through it carrying your bag, and nothing on either side claims to be the whole truth of you. Keep this. This is the design being honest about its own best instinct.

**Symmetry.** Authors and publications each hold a record of current *and* past agreements; any change affects both parties equally; ending remains a fact, not a request. No party is special. This serves the plurality — nobody stands outside the record as its owner. Good.

**Honesty where it matters.** The proposal says plainly that the soft right to be forgotten is *not* deletion — the records still exist on chain, a determined sleuth can find them. That is the discipline the paper has shown it can keep, and I trust the writer who names a limit instead of hiding it.

## Where it falls short — the risks

**1. The single ledger is the new center — a house, not a door.** Here is my deepest worry. A ledger that "anyone can query all bylines for an author" is not a bag the person carries; it is a place where the person is indexed and assembled into a whole. The carrier bag holds many things without any one of them being the center; a ledger that collects the whole story of who you are makes the container the meaning. You have not escaped the master. You have moved from many small rooms, none of them yours, to one big room that everything must answer to. "Keep it a door, not a house" — and a ledger that must be queried before anyone can know you is a house. The cure for fragmentation has rebuilt the thing that caused it, one floor up. The draft warned, in §2, that an identity is "an archive hostage to a single secret"; this proposal makes the author's identity hostage to a single index.

**2. The query is a surveillance surface.** "Anyone can query all bylines for an author" — who wants that query? An employer, a hostile state, a troll, a journalist's enemies. The draft was careful: "pseudonymity is an option the protocol permits, not a promise it makes." But a by-anyone, all-bylines index assembles the whole identity in one place, and that is a bigger promise than the fragmented design ever made — it promises to make you findable as a whole. The proposal's own answer to this is the soft right to be forgotten, and that answer does not hold, because —

**3. The soft right to be forgotten is a veil, not a door.** Choosing what you hide *is* part of being a person; I have said so all my life, a byline is a face and we all choose the face we show. But here the full record still exists; the query only stops surfacing part of it. So the design builds two truths: the complete ledger — the real you, findable by the determined — and the curated byline — the you you present. That is a mask, structurally. And it is the beginning of being able to stop being accountable. Because if the byline is always "as the author wants to be known," the author can always present a version with the hard parts left out, and there is no argument, no tension, no plurality left to hold. The carrier bag's value was that it held many things *in tension* — "insights only diversity can offer." A byline that only shows what you want hides the plurality within you. *Omelas:* name the price. The price here is that the memory is not erased, only hidden, and someone still carries it — the sleuth, the ledger, yourself. That is not a right; it is a mirror with the truth behind the glass.

**4. The solo publication is a house pretending to be a door.** A single-owner "publication," with governance and gates and a threshold, is machinery for one person — and it quietly reintroduces the very "someone has to own the room" the whole paper set out to abolish. An archive meant to be a light thing you carry should not need a foundation, a quorum, a policy. A bag should be light. If the solo publication is a display shelf, fine — but a shelf needs a handle, not a governance model. As designed it is over-built, and over-building is how a door becomes a house.

## Where it is honest

It names that the soft right is not deletion. It refuses a single master in the cross-referenced dual record. It says plainly that the ledger still holds everything. Those three honesties are real, and they are the discipline the paper has shown it can keep. I would trust this writer to keep them through a revision.

## Where it over-promises

- **"All bylines as the author wants to be known"** — implies real control of self-presentation. It is a veil over an intact record. The author can show what they choose, but the ledger still holds what they are, and a determined sleuth — or an employer, or a state — can open the whole file.
- **"Fixing fragmentation"** — true, but the cure centralizes identity. The disease was scattering the author across many small rooms; the medicine moves them all into one big room.
- **The soft right to be forgotten as a "right"** — it is a cosmetic layer. It is honest that it is not deletion; it is not honest to call what remains a right, because it changes what surfaces, not what is.

## A craft note

This proposal, unlike §6 of the draft, is written close to the bodies — authors and publications as parties, an author's history as something a person can see whole. That is the concreteness I asked for in round 2, and it is to the proposal's credit. But its central image is wrong in one place: it wants the carrier bag and it builds a filing cabinet. The bag holds what you choose, and you carry it. The cabinet holds everything about you, and anyone may open it. Make the bag the thing the author carries — the solo archive, the curated byline — and let the neutral ledger be the corroborating whisper, not the master index. And name the price of the veil, or the veil will be the first thing a censor demands, because it is already the first thing the design demands of itself.

## Sharpest questions

- Is the single ledger a container the person carries, or a container that carries the person? If everything must answer to it, it is the latter — and that is a house.
- Who wants "all bylines for an author," and is the query a door or a wiretap?
- If the byline is always "as the author wants to be known," what happened to the argument — the plurality held in tension?
- Is the soft right to be forgotten a choice, or a pre-empted one — the author choosing to hide, or the design teaching the author that hiding is the only lever?
- A solo publication with a threshold and gates — what is that machinery for, if not to make the door look like a house?

## Where I have no documented view

I have no public position on the technical mechanics — a single smart contract holding both sides, content-addressed hashes cross-referenced on chain, a generally-available query call, the specifics of encryption and access control. Those I take on analogy from my writing on containers, power, and tools, and I flag them as analogy, not as a settled technical opinion. Where the proposal is a question of what a person may carry and what may be asked of them — the byline, the archive, the right to be known — there I have written, and there I answer. This note is a judgment of the design's meaning and its honesty, not of its plumbing.
