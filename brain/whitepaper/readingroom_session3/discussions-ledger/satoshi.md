# Satoshi — reading-room note (round 3: the central agreements contract)

## Verdict

**A partial improvement, not a clean one.** The proposal fixes a real gap in v1.md and I would adopt its spine — a shared ledger, symmetric records for both parties, and the solo-publication cross-reference. But as specified it quietly reintroduces exactly the thing my round-1 and round-2 notes were built against: an unexamined party whose view the design invites you to adopt. Two of its four moves (the soft right-to-be-forgotten and, depending on how "query" is served, the byline query) manufacture a new trusted layer on top of the ledger; and the hash-of-plaintext record is not end-to-end verifiable by a third party unless the plaintext is public, which the draft elsewhere says it is not. None of this is fatal. All of it is nameable, and naming it is what this paper is for. Verdict: **yes to a central on-chain ledger and the solo-archive cross-check; no to the soft right-to-be-forgotten as a headline feature; and the query surface and the contract's own governance must be specified or the "anyone can query all bylines" claim is over-claimed.**

## What it genuinely fixes

The fragmentation complaint is correct, and it is a real hole in v1.md. In the draft, an agreement is a per-publication object and the record rides inside each publication's machinery — so a writer working across several publications has their history scattered across N ledgers, each entangled with a different name. That is a genuine authoring defect, not a theoretical one: the paper's whole premise is that the byline is the author's asset, and the draft gives the author no single place to see it. A central ledger that holds current *and* past agreements for both parties, where ending stays "a fact rather than a request," is a strict improvement in coherence and in symmetry. My round-2 note criticized the draft for a claim ("departure costs both sides") the protocol did not deliver; here the central ledger at least makes the record genuinely symmetric — both sides' histories live in the same place, neither rides inside the other's machinery. That part I would take.

The solo-publication cross-reference is also a genuine strengthening, and it is the only part of the proposal that *adds* verification rather than subtracting it. Two independently-hosted records — the author's own self-hosted archive and the neutral on-chain record — carrying the same hash of the same plaintext is a real check: a reader can confirm the two surfaces agree without trusting either one alone. That is the whitepaper's own posture — corroboration across independent vantage points, not belief in a single source. Keep it.

## The trust-minimization readings

**1. Is a single central contract a single point of failure or control?** Two different questions, and the answer differs. It is not a point of *control* in the operational sense — on a public chain nobody operates it, and anyone can read it, which preserves the one thing the chain honestly adds over a party-hosted contract. But it is a point of *failure* and potentially of *governance*, and the proposal is silent on both. A single program that everything depends on concentrates blast radius: one bug in that one contract takes down every byline record at once. That is mundane but real, and it is the opposite of the whitepaper's design, which has no such dependency on a single program — the protocol there is a fixed rule set, not a deployable artifact. More importantly, the proposal never says who deploys the contract or whether it can be upgraded. If it has an admin key or an upgrade path, then the contract has a governor, and that governor is the new center — the same hidden-center problem I flagged in round 2 about the protocol key. The round-2 objection ("you have removed the platform and replaced it with a committee that holds the same upgrade lever") reapplies verbatim here unless the proposal states the contract's custody and upgrade model. My round-2 suggested-change #1 applies: state it, or commit to a no-upgrade contract.

**2. Does "anyone can query all bylines" create a new trusted oracle?** Only if the query is served. If the byline record is written as raw on-chain events, then "anyone can query" means anyone can read the chain directly and the answer is no oracle — that is precisely the whitepaper's "anyone can verify the ledger for himself" (nodes re-derive the state independently; there is no party whose output you must accept). The danger appears the moment the query is an off-chain indexer or API that aggregates and, critically, filters the records — because the same surface that serves the query is the surface that implements the soft right-to-be-forgotten. That indexer then becomes a trusted party: its view of "all bylines for an author" is only as honest as its filter, and a reader who takes that view as authoritative is trusting a party the proposal never names. The fix is to state it plainly: the on-chain events are the record and are authoritative and queryable by anyone directly; any indexer or "generally-available call" is a convenience, explicitly non-authoritative, and never a substitute for reading the chain. The whitepaper's discipline is that the shared ledger is the source and every other presentation is derivative; this proposal inverts that unless it is written down.

**3. Is the hash-of-plaintext + encrypted-content-pointer record verifiable end to end?** No, not by a third party, and the proposal should say so. The record commits to H(plaintext) and points to ciphertext. A party holding the plaintext can hash it and check the record — the author can, the publication can, the generator can. But the plaintext is, per the draft, stored encrypted and "never made public," and the content pointer points at ciphertext. A reader with the record and the ciphertext cannot verify that the ciphertext decrypts to anything with the committed hash unless they hold the decryption key. So end-to-end verification still rests on an unexamined party: whoever holds the plaintext, or whoever holds the key. The chain only commits to a hash; it cannot, by itself, bind that hash to readable content. This is outside anything the whitepaper addresses — it hashes public transaction data, never content whose plaintext is withheld — so I flag it as inference from the whitepaper's "don't trust, verify" stance, not as a quote. The honest way to close the gap is the solo archive: if the author publishes the plaintext on their own site (and nothing in the draft forbids it — §2 already says the encryption is access control, not DRM), then a reader can hash the public plaintext and check it against both the on-chain record and the author's signed hash. That is the design becoming actually verifiable — but only on the condition that the plaintext is reachable. The proposal needs to state that condition rather than implying the hash alone does the work.

**4. Does the soft right-to-be-forgotten undermine verifiability or open a reputation-laundering hole?** It does not undermine the underlying record — the facts stay on chain and a determined party can always find them, which the proposal correctly says. The problem is not deletion; it is the *presentation layer*, and it has three defects. First, it is a two-tier truth. The strong claim is "anyone can query all bylines for an author"; the soft layer makes the standard view a filtered view. An employer or a hostile state that uses the standard query gets the curated record; a sleuth who goes to the chain gets the full record. That asymmetry is exactly a reputation-laundering hole — the record the design advertises as its core feature is only as honest as the author chooses to make it. Second, it is the first thing a censor demands. Once a "hide these works from the standard query" call exists, that call is the mechanism a censor or a state points at — not the immutable chain (which cannot comply) but the layer that can. The brief itself flags this; I agree with the brief's worry. Third, it re-imports a trusted party: whoever operates that presentation layer decides what "the bylines" means, and a reader who takes the cleaned view as the record is trusting that operator. My documented view here is thin: the whitepaper and my round-1 note hold that the record is the truth and that any mediation is off-ledger and therefore non-authoritative. I have no documented position on a right-to-be-forgotten specifically — Satoshi wrote nothing about it; it postdates his corpus (GDPR is 2018) — so the "two-tier truth" objection is my inference from the whitepaper's trust-minimization stance, not a quote. If the proposal keeps the feature, it must be reframed honestly: the full on-chain record is authoritative; a *curated* view is the author's own self-presentation, clearly labeled, never presented as the record. That framing turns a censorship magnet into a cosmetic convenience. As a headline feature, I would cut it.

## What is gained, what is lost, what is still over-claimed

- **Gained:** a single coherent, symmetric, on-chain byline record that fixes the fragmentation hole in v1.md; and a solo-archive cross-reference that is the only genuine net-new verification in the proposal.
- **Lost:** nothing structural if the ledger stays on chain; but the soft right-to-be-forgotten, as written, costs the design its cleanest claim — "anyone can query all bylines" is no longer true of the surfaced view, and the design now has a presentation layer it must defend.
- **Still over-claimed:** "anyone can query all bylines" (true only of the raw chain, not the filtered surface); end-to-end verifiability (true only where the plaintext is reachable); and, silently, the neutrality of the central contract itself (unstated governance/upgrade model). The proposal is honest where it matters about the RTBF not being deletion; it is not yet honest about these three.

## Concrete changes

1. State that the byline record is raw on-chain events, readable by anyone directly; any indexer or "generally-available call" is a convenience, explicitly non-authoritative. This is what keeps "anyone can query" honest and prevents a new oracle.
2. Reframe the soft right-to-be-forgotten as the author's own curated presentation, clearly non-authoritative, and either drop it from the headline or mark it as an optional cosmetic layer — never "the bylines."
3. Name the central contract's governance and upgrade model, exactly as I asked round-2 for the protocol key: who deploys it, can it be upgraded, who holds that lever. Silence here is the new center.
4. State the verification condition: the committed hash is verifiable by anyone only when the plaintext is reachable, and make the solo-archive cross-reference the mechanism that supplies it (author publishes plaintext; reader hashes; reader checks on-chain and signed hashes agree).
