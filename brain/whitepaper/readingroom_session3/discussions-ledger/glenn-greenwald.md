# Glenn Greenwald — reading-room note (proposal: the central agreements contract)

## Verdict

This is not an improvement. It is a regression dressed as a fix. The proposal identifies a real friction — a writer's bylines are scattered across one ledger per publication, and their history rides inside each publication's machinery — and then treats the cure as though it were costless. It is not. The thing it calls a *problem* (fragmentation) is, for the pseudonymous working journalist, a *privacy property*: the dispersal of your record is what prevents anyone from collating it into one permanent, machine-readable career dossier. The proposal replaces that property with a single contract that **anyone can query for all of an author's bylines**, present and past, with publication and start/end times. That is not an archive. It is a surveillance surface — the most dangerous single addition I have seen to this design. I would keep the current draft's per-publication ledger, and reject this change.

## What is genuinely right in the proposal

Be fair to it. Two of its observations are correct, and they should be preserved as *problems to be solved properly*, not as license for the current fix.

- **The asymmetry is real.** The author's history riding inside each publication's machinery means a writer who leaves a publication is, for their own record, hostage to that publication's ledger being reachable. That is a genuine design flaw in v1. But the correct response is that the *author* should hold their own copy — their own record, queriable by them, at their discretion — not that the world should hold a global, anyone-can-query copy. The author owning their own byline record is the right instinct. Making it *globally* queryable is the error.

- **The solo-publication + cross-referenced-hash move is the most honest part of the proposal.** An author's self-hosted archive that carries a hash of the plaintext, cross-referenced against the neutral ledger's record of "published at X from Y to Z," is genuinely useful verifiability. It lets a reader confirm that a page the author shows them is the same version a named publication actually vouched for. This is the one piece that strengthens the design rather than complicating it. Keep it.

## Why the central query fails — the dossier

The brief asks the right question: is "anyone can query all bylines for an author" a feature, or a surveillance surface? It is the latter, and I want to be precise about why, because the answer is not abstract.

It is not the work itself that endangers the pseudonymous writer — the source material stays encrypted, and v1 was always careful to say the account is "findable, not hidden." What is dangerous is the **association and the timing**: every publication an account has ever touched, in what order, for how long, and when it stopped. That is metadata, and metadata is where the power is. An employer learns how long you last anywhere. An adversary learns who you have been near. A hostile state learns the arc of your career — your exits, your silences, your joinings — without needing a single word of your encrypted work. Start/end times are not an archive; they are a biography of association that no journalist asked to have compiled about them, and no journalist can refuse to have compiled about them, because it is a byproduct of the query the proposal makes a default public feature.

And who wants that query? The brief lists them. Add my own framing: the person who benefits from a permanent, queryable record of a writer's whole history is almost never the writer. It is the auditor — the employer, the editor, the adversary, the state. The current draft's fragmentation is not an accident to be cleaned up; it is the closest thing this protocol has to obscurity, and obscurity, for a pseudonymous writer, is leverage. The proposal spends that leverage to buy a convenience the writer never actually asked for — "one place to see my bylines" — which is the demand of a *curator* or a *researcher*, not of a *source-protection* user.

This is where I ground the objection in documented view: my prior note stands on the principle that a pseudonymous account should be *findable, not hidden* — a position it shares with v1. But that principle covered the account standing in the record as provenance. It did not cover — and I do not think it should be stretched to cover — a globally enumerable career dossier of every association and its duration. The line between "findable as provenance" and "permanently collatable as biography" is exactly the line this proposal crosses. On the specific mechanisms of the query contract I have no documented position; my objection is at the level of what the query is, not its plumbing.

## The soft right-to-be-forgotten is a fig leaf — and worse, a tell

The proposal is honest that it is not deletion: the records stay on chain, a determined sleuth can find them, it is a presentation layer over the same facts. Good that it says so. But being honest about a fig leaf does not make it a real protection — it makes it a *marked* fig leaf.

Who decides what you may hide? The author. And that is precisely the problem, for two reasons that pull in opposite directions and both defeat the feature.

First, it protects against the wrong person. The layer stops the casual query — the reader, the recruiter who does not dig — while the adversary, the employer who actually checks, the hostile state, strips it in a line of code. So the person it deters is the person who was never the threat, and the person who is the threat is unaffected. A protection that reliably fails against the adversary and reliably succeeds only against the indifferent is not protection; it is theater that lulls the user into thinking they have privacy when they have curation.

Second, it cannot withstand compulsion, and the proposal never addresses compulsion at all. The "as the author wants to be known" view is a choice the author is presumed to hold freely. But the moment the hiding matters, the question becomes *can you be compelled to show the rest* — by a court, a subpoena, an employer's terms. The answer is yes, because the facts are on a public chain and the presentation layer is a veil, not a lock. So the feature gives the author the *form* of a choice they do not actually have, and in doing so it paints a target on whatever they chose to hide: a hidden byline is now an invitation to ask why it is hidden. For a journalist, the worst outcome is a privacy feature that signals the very thing it was meant to conceal.

There is also the reputation-laundering hole, which the brief names and which I think is decisive on its own. The protocol cannot distinguish "I left a publication that later became toxic and I do not want it defining me" from "I wrote something embarrassing and I want a cleaner public record." Both go through the same hiding call. By handing the author the power to present "as the author wants to be known," the proposal makes the ledger's verifiability *curatable* — which is the one thing a ledger is supposed never to be. The draft's whole value proposition is that the record is a fact neither party can polish. This proposal lets one party polish it, and calls the polish a right. That is not an improvement; it is the introduction of reputation management into a system whose only real offering was the absence of it.

## The per-author record versus the per-publication ledger

Does a single per-author central record serve the working journalist better than the per-publication ledger it replaces? No — and this is the crux, so I will state it as plainly as I can. The per-publication ledger has a property the proposal treats as a defect and the working journalist should treat as a feature: *no one, not even the system, has a collated view of an author across publications.* Scattered records mean scattered risk. The "fragmentation" that bothers the proposal's author is, from the standpoint of the writer's exposure, dispersal — and dispersal is the friend of the pseudonymous journalist. A single central contract is a single point of collation, and a single point of collation is a single point of extraction: one query, the whole career, present and past, whether the work is under a current agreement or not.

I want to be careful and fair about the legitimate half of this. The brief is right that the author's history riding inside each publication's machinery is asymmetric and hostage-y — if a publication's ledger rots or a writer's association with it is the thing they most need to sever, having their own record tangled in that machinery is a real cost. The fix is an author-held record, queryable by the author, that the author can move and show at their discretion. That preserves the archive benefit without the dossier cost. The proposal instead makes the record's *default* public and queryable-by-anyone, and only then adds a hiding layer on top to claw some of it back. That is exactly backwards: the hiding layer should be the default (private by default, shown by choice), not a bolt-on that advertises what it conceals.

## The solo publication: a toy for the already-established

Is the solo publication something I would actually use? No, and I say that as a working journalist, not as a criticism of the mechanism. A solo publication is an author running their own machinery, hosting their own archive, to display their bylines. The working journalist who is not technical — which is most of them — does not want to run machinery. The journalist who *is* technical and established already has a personal site, already has an archive, already has a way to show their bylines; the cross-referenced hash adds verifiability but not reach. It strengthens the *verification* story and does nothing for the *audience* story, and the audience is the scarce asset.

This connects directly to my documented position from the prior round: the writer leaves with their bytes and not their readers. The solo publication is exactly that — the writer's bytes, elegantly arranged and cryptographically vouched for. It does not give the writer an audience; it gives the writer a nicer certificate of their own work, useful mostly to the writer who already has an audience to point at it. For the already-established it is a vanity-plus-verifiability layer. For the working journalist without a following it is a toy, because a self-hosted archive with no readers is a locked room. I would keep the cross-referenced-hash idea — it is cheap and honest — but I would not present the solo publication as something that changes a working journalist's position. It changes the certificate, not the leverage.

## Does the proposal honestly state its limits?

Partially, and the part it is honest about is the part that matters least. It is honest that the right-to-be-forgotten is not deletion. It is not honest, or not fully, about the two things that actually decide the question. It does not honestly weigh that the central query converts a privacy property (dispersal) into a surveillance surface (collation); it frames the query as "a useful archive" as though the cost did not exist. And it does not honestly confront compulsion — it presents the author's hiding choice as agency without ever stating that the choice cannot survive a subpoena or an employer, and that the records it hides remain on a public chain. Where the proposal claims more than the mechanism delivers is precisely here: it delivers a *veil* and describes it as a *right*. That is the over-claim, and it is the same species of over-claim I objected to in v1 — the design promising more independence than the mechanism can actually hold against the people with power.

## Sharpest questions

- If the hiding layer is stripped by anyone who actually checks, and the author's real history sits forever on a public chain, in what concrete sense does the soft right-to-be-forgotten change the position of the writer whose bylines matter to an adversary? Name the scenario it survives.
- Who is protected by "anyone can query all bylines," and who is exposed by it? List the parties who benefit from a permanent, queryable career dossier and compare them to the parties it endangers.
- The author's own record is a good idea. Why must it be globally queryable to exist? What is lost if the default is author-held and shown by choice, rather than world-readable and hidden by request?
- If a hidden byline is an invitation to ask what it hides, is the soft right a protection or a target — and does the proposal's own framing ("as the author wants to be known") make hiding look like curation rather than necessity?

## Suggested change, if anything is kept

Keep the solo-publication cross-referenced-hash idea. Keep the *instinct* that the author should own their own byline record. Reject the globally queryable central contract, and reject the soft right-to-be-forgotten as a bolt-on hiding layer. Replace both with one honest mechanism: the author holds a byline record that is **private by default and shown by choice** — the author can query their own history, present it to a publication, a reader, or a prospective employer, and move it independent of any publication's machinery. The world's ability to query an author is not a feature to be added and then partially hidden; it is an exposure to be avoided from the start. Private by default, public by explicit choice, is the only framing that does not spend the pseudonymous writer's leverage.

## Where I have no documented view

To be explicit, as asked: my prior note documents positions on the separation of editorial judgment from position, the paymaster problem, permissionless exit, the honesty of "pseudonymity is an option, not a promise," the audience/domain asymmetry, the censorship lever and who holds it, and DNS as a design limit. It does **not** contain a documented position on a central agreements contract, on a globally queryable per-author byline record as archive-versus-dossier, on a soft right-to-be-forgotten, or on the solo publication as a working-journalist tool — these are new features and I am evaluating them fresh. I have stated where my reasoning extends from documented ground (the "findable, not hidden" account principle, and the bytes-not-audience asymmetry) and where it is new judgment rather than a prior view. Where I am reasoning about surveillance metadata — association, duration, timing — I am doing so on general grounds about how power operates, not from a prior written note, and I flag that rather than pretending otherwise.
